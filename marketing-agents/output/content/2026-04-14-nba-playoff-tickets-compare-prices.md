## Same seat, different price

Round 1 of the NBA Playoffs is in full swing. One morning this week, the same lower-bowl seat at TD Garden was listed at $140 on one platform and $95 on another. A $45 difference. Same view. Same armrests. Same overpriced beer.

This happens every April. Playoff demand spikes, resale inventory floods in from season ticket holders, and three or four platforms end up listing the same seats at very different prices. The fans who check one site pay more. The fans who check three don't.

You don't need to spend your afternoon on browser tabs to be one of the latter. Five things matter.

## Pick your section, then compare that section everywhere

Start with Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub. Those three cover roughly 90% of NBA resale inventory.

Don't start by filtering for "cheapest available." That usually means obstructed view, last row of the upper deck, or a single seat with nobody next to you. Pick the section and row you actually want, then look up that specific location across all three platforms.

On TicketScan, you search once and see prices from all three side by side. But even if you do it manually, the principle is the same: compare apples to apples. Same section, same row range, same number of seats.

## Fees will trick you if you let them

The sticker price on SeatGeek might look $30 cheaper than StubHub. Then fees show up at checkout and that gap vanishes or flips entirely.

Rough fee ranges for NBA playoff resale: Ticketmaster tacks on 15-20%. StubHub runs 10-15% but lets you toggle fee-inclusive pricing. SeatGeek is 12-18%, hidden until the final screen by default.

So a $150 listing on SeatGeek might actually cost $174. That same section on StubHub at $165 all-in? It was the better deal the whole time, and you'd never know from the search results page.

Always compare the checkout price, not the listing price. TicketScan pulls fee-inclusive numbers where possible so you're comparing what you'll actually pay.

## The pre-game price dip is your friend

Ticket prices follow a pattern. They spike when the matchup is announced, stay elevated for a few days, then drop in the hours before the game as sellers with unsold inventory get nervous and buyers with cold feet dump theirs.

This dip is strongest for Play-In Tournament games, weeknight games, and Games 1 and 2 of a first-round series. Lower perceived stakes, fewer last-minute impulse buyers.

The window is roughly 4 to 6 hours before tip-off. If you can stomach waiting, this is when the platform price gaps get widest and the best deals surface.

## Set an alert and stop refreshing

You don't need to sit there hitting reload. Set a target price on TicketScan's watchlist and we'll ping you when any platform drops below it.

Pick a target that's grounded in something real. Look at what lower-bowl tickets actually sold for in last year's playoffs at the same venue, then knock 10-15% off that. That gives you a number based on market data instead of vibes.

Some rough starting points based on early 2026 first-round listings:

TD Garden (Celtics) runs $85 upper deck, $190 lower bowl. Madison Square Garden (Knicks) is the priciest at $120 upper, $280 lower. Crypto.com Arena (Lakers) sits around $95 and $210. Chase Center (Warriors) is surprisingly the cheapest of these four at $80 and $175.

These numbers shift daily. The point is having a baseline so you can recognize a genuinely good price when one appears.

## Check face value before you go resale

Not every team sells out instantly. The Sabres (NHL, but same idea) put face-value playoff tickets on general sale this week and they're still available. Smaller-market NBA teams sometimes have direct-sale inventory days into a series.

Check the team's official Ticketmaster page first. If face-value tickets exist, they beat resale after fees almost every time.

## How this changes as the bracket advances

We're mid-way through Round 1 as this publishes. Round 1 is your best comparison shopping window by far: most inventory, widest price gaps between platforms, most nervous sellers. By the Conference Finals, inventory tightens and prices across platforms converge. Less room to find an edge. If you're buying for a later round, lock in your baseline target price now — platforms don't start converging until the series narrows.

## Quick reference

1. Pick your section first, then compare that section across Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub
2. Add fees to every price before comparing (10-20% depending on platform)
3. If you can wait, check again 4-6 hours before tip-off
4. Set a price alert so you don't have to refresh manually
5. Check face-value availability before going to the resale market

That $45 gap from this morning? It's real, it's common, and it goes to whoever checks more than one website.
