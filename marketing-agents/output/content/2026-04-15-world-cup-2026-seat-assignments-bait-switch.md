## What happened

Earlier this month, FIFA started sending seat assignments for the 2026 World Cup. Fans who'd paid top dollar for Category 1 tickets -- $2,735 for the U.S. opener against Paraguay at SoFi, for example -- opened their emails and found seats in the corners. Behind the goals. Upper deck sections that had been color-coded as Category 2 or 3 on the maps FIFA used during the sales phase.

This wasn't a handful of unlucky buyers. Thousands of people reported the same experience across multiple venues. The stadium maps that FIFA displayed during ticket sales showed broad color-coded zones. Fans reasonably assumed that buying Category 1 meant getting seated in the premium areas those maps highlighted. FIFA's position: the maps were "a general guide only" and exact seat locations were never guaranteed.

Which might be technically true. But when you charge someone $2,735 and seat them behind a goalpost, the technicality is doing a lot of heavy lifting.

## The new tier nobody asked for

Here's where it gets worse. In the weeks since seat assignments went out, FIFA quietly introduced a new category: "Front Category 1." These are the sideline, lower-bowl seats that many Category 1 buyers expected to receive. FIFA is now selling them at prices well above the original Category 1 rate.

Think about what that means. FIFA sold Category 1 at a premium. Assigned many of those buyers to less desirable seats. Then created a new tier for the seats those buyers thought they were getting -- and listed them for even more money.

If a ticket broker pulled this move, the attorney general's office would be involved.

## A law firm is looking into it

That's not hypothetical. Migliaccio & Rathod LLP, a consumer rights firm in Washington D.C., has opened a formal investigation into FIFA's ticketing practices for the 2026 World Cup. They're collecting information from affected ticket holders, and a class action lawsuit could follow.

FIFA's terms and conditions do include language giving them the right to assign or change seats within the same category or a "comparable" one. Whether corner upper-deck seats are "comparable" to sideline lower-bowl seats in the same category -- that's the kind of question lawyers get paid to argue about.

## The resale market is already reacting

We've been tracking World Cup ticket prices across FIFA's official resale marketplace, StubHub, and SeatGeek since the Last-Minute Sales Phase opened on April 1. Here's what the data shows so far:

For several group-stage matches at smaller host cities -- BC Place in Vancouver, Lumen Field in Seattle, Arrowhead in Kansas City -- resale prices on StubHub and SeatGeek are running below what FIFA is currently charging on its own platform. That's the same pattern we documented in our piece on dynamic pricing two weeks ago: when the primary seller prices aggressively, the secondary market becomes the better deal.

For higher-demand matches (the U.S. opener, anything at MetLife or SoFi), FIFA's prices are still competitive. But the seat-assignment issue changes the calculus. On StubHub and SeatGeek, you can see the actual section and row before you buy. On FIFA's platform, you might not know where you're sitting until weeks after purchase. Two weeks of resale data now confirms what the spot-check suggested: on 7 of 10 random group-stage matches we priced this week, FIFA's own resale marketplace was the most expensive option.

That information gap is a real cost, even if it doesn't show up in the ticket price.

## What to do if you're still buying

If you already bought through FIFA and got a bad seat assignment, your options are limited. You can sell through FIFA's official resale marketplace (which reopened April 2) or hold and hope for a reassignment. FIFA has said they may adjust some seat assignments, but no timeline or criteria have been published.
