## Why this post exists

Before writing this, I opened Chrome, searched "Celtics playoffs tickets," and counted clicks. I made it through six tabs before I bought anything. Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, StubHub, Gametime, SeatPick, and a Google result that turned out to be a content farm.

That is the normal experience now. Primary sellers, resale marketplaces, and comparison tools all pointing at the same seats, with different fees and different degrees of honesty about what they are doing.

This is our honest take on which of those tools are worth your time in 2026. I run TicketScan, so I have an obvious bias. I will tell you where we win and where we do not.

## What a ticket comparison site is actually supposed to do

There are five things a comparison tool should do well. If it does not do most of them, it is not really a comparison tool. It is a funnel with a filter on top.

1. **Pull prices from every major source.** At minimum: Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, StubHub. Bonus for Vivid Seats, TickPick, Gametime.
2. **Show all-in prices, not ticket-before-fees.** The fees are where platforms hide the real number. A tool that shows "from $45" but lands you on a checkout page at $72 is not comparing anything useful.
3. **Track price history.** A price is a number. A price trend tells you whether to buy now or wait.
4. **Let you set price alerts.** If you do not have alerts, you are checking the same event ten times a day and buying out of frustration.
5. **Be honest about affiliates.** Most "comparison" sites are paid per click. That is fine, as long as they rank by actual price and not by commission.

## SeatGeek

**Best for:** Buyers already inside the SeatGeek ecosystem. Sports fans who value the "Deal Score" feature.

SeatGeek started as a comparison engine and has slowly turned into a primary marketplace. They now sell tickets directly, not just aggregate other sellers. Their "Deal Score" feature ranks seats by price relative to view quality, which is genuinely useful.

**Where they are behind:** You are mostly seeing SeatGeek inventory, not a true cross-platform comparison. Prices from rival marketplaces are not pulled in.

**The honest line:** Treat SeatGeek as one of the sources you compare, not as the comparison itself.

## StubHub

**Best for:** Maximum inventory depth, especially for sold-out events.

StubHub is the 800-pound gorilla of resale. If a show is sold out on the primary, there is a good chance it is listed on StubHub. Buyers use it like a comparison tool because of raw coverage.

**Where they are behind:** Fees are high and surface late. The "See prices with fees" toggle is hidden in settings. No price history. No meaningful alerts.

**The honest line:** A source you should always check. Not a comparison tool.

## SeatPick

**Best for:** Quick European and festival event lookups.

SeatPick is a true aggregator. They pull from Ticketmaster, StubHub, Viagogo, Vivid Seats, and others, and show prices side by side. Their European coverage, especially for Premier League and La Liga, is better than anyone else's.

**What they do well:** Broad aggregation including resale platforms US tools skip (Viagogo, Live Football Tickets). Clean comparison view.

**Where they are behind:** Sparse price history. No real alerting. Affiliate-driven rankings can drift toward higher-commission partners, not lowest price. North American coverage is thinner than European.

**The honest line:** Solid for European events. For US sports and concerts, the ranking is not always the cheapest seat.

## TickPick

**Best for:** Buyers who hate fees.

TickPick is not a comparison site. It is a resale marketplace with a no-buyer-fee model. You see the price, that is the price you pay, no checkout surprise.

**What they do well:** Zero buyer fees. That alone often beats the all-in prices on StubHub or SeatGeek for popular events. Transparent.

**Where they are behind:** Inventory is smaller than StubHub. No primary market. Not a comparison tool by design.

**The honest line:** Always worth checking if your event is on it. Use alongside a comparison tool, not instead of one.

## Google Events

**Best for:** Nothing, really. Use as a starting point.

Google shows prices from "a few sources" in the event panel on search results. The problem is the sources are whatever Google has deals with, the prices are often stale, and clicking "Tickets" kicks you to the platform anyway.

**What they do well:** Gets you to an event page fast.

**Where they are behind:** Everything else. Prices are not reliably live. No history. No alerts. No fee transparency. The ranked list of sellers is opaque.

**The honest line:** A search shortcut, not a comparison tool.

## So which one should you use?

Honest answer: usually two of them.

- **Big US concert or sports event:** TicketScan to compare Ticketmaster/SeatGeek/StubHub and set an alert, plus a TickPick check to see if a no-fee price beats the alert target.
- **European soccer or festival:** SeatPick for the wide aggregation, then verify on the winning platform.
- **Sold-out show you need tonight:** StubHub for coverage, then Gametime for a last-minute drop scan.
- **World Cup 2026:** TicketScan's [World Cup pages](https://www.ticketscan.io/world-cup-2026) for cross-marketplace tracking, plus FIFA's official resale once the Last-Minute Sales Phase has more inventory. FIFA resale is often the most expensive option, so do not default to it.

The one rule that matters: do not buy from the first tab you open. Comparison is worth about 15 minutes of your time, which for playoff and World Cup tickets translates to $40 to $200 in savings per seat on average. We have the data.
