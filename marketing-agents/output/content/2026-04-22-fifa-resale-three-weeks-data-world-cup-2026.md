## The question we set out to answer

FIFA opened its official resale marketplace on April 2. The pitch from FIFA's comms team was that going through "official" channels would mean fairer prices, verified inventory, and protection against the fraud you see on grey-market sites. The implication being that fans who cared about getting a straight deal should stick with FIFA rather than use StubHub or SeatGeek.

That's a testable claim. So we tested it.

For the last 20 days we've been pricing the same group-stage World Cup matches across three marketplaces at the same time, three times a day: FIFA's official resale, StubHub, and SeatGeek. We fixed the comparison to Category 3 seats (or the closest equivalent section on StubHub and SeatGeek, which use section-and-row rather than FIFA's category system). We excluded hospitality packages. We included all fees in the final price. And we only counted a match if all three platforms had live listings for comparable seats at the same timestamp.

That filter gave us 33 group-stage matches with enough data to compare. Here's what the data says.

## The headline number

On 22 of the 33 matches (66.7%), FIFA's official resale was more expensive than the cheaper of StubHub and SeatGeek. The median spread on those 22: $84 per ticket. The biggest single outlier was a Brazil group-stage match at Hard Rock Stadium in Miami: FIFA resale was listing Category 3 at $612 fees-in, while StubHub had the same-level seat at $331. A $281 gap on a single ticket, on the same day, for the same view.

Not every match went that way. FIFA was the cheapest option on 6 matches, and roughly tied on 5 more. So the spread isn't one-sided. But the pattern is clear enough that the "official means fairer" framing doesn't hold up when you actually check.

If you bought Category 3 at face value during the Last-Minute Sales Phase and wanted to compare your ticket against the resale alternative, you'd have been better off with the non-FIFA option roughly two times out of three.

## Where FIFA actually wins

The 6 matches where FIFA was cheapest are more interesting than the headline number, because they tell you *where* to check FIFA first.

Three of the six are at Mexican host venues: Estadio Azteca in Mexico City, Estadio Akron in Guadalajara, and Estadio BBVA in Monterrey. Two are at BC Place in Vancouver. One is at BMO Field in Toronto.

There's a clean explanation for that. Mexican and Canadian host cities have thinner secondary-market supply. StubHub and SeatGeek are primarily US-focused platforms; their inventory depth for matches in those cities is a fraction of what it is for US matches. When resale supply is thin, prices on those platforms drift up toward the FIFA listing rather than undercutting it. And FIFA's own listings in those markets appear to be priced against local fan-buying capacity rather than the dynamic-pricing ceiling we've documented at SoFi and MetLife.

So the practical rule is this: if your match is at Azteca, Akron, BBVA, BC Place, or BMO Field, check FIFA first. If it's anywhere in the United States, check FIFA last.

## Where FIFA loses badly

The 22 matches where the non-FIFA platforms were cheaper cluster around the US-opener and finals-contender venues. SoFi, MetLife, AT&T, Hard Rock, Mercedes-Benz. These are the same venues where we saw the most aggressive dynamic pricing behavior on the primary market during the April 1 price hike, and the pattern has continued into resale.

Three specific numbers from this morning's pull, all Category 3 equivalent, all fees-in, all captured within the same 15-minute window:

One of the Argentina group-stage matches at AT&T Stadium was $488 on FIFA resale, $389 on SeatGeek, and $401 on StubHub. A $99 gap from FIFA to the cheapest option.

A Germany match at Mercedes-Benz Stadium was $524 on FIFA, $427 on StubHub, and $445 on SeatGeek. A $97 gap.

The England opener at MetLife was $756 on FIFA, $619 on StubHub, and $648 on SeatGeek. A $137 gap.

Three matches, three US host venues, three wins for the non-FIFA option. Median savings across all 22: $84. Top-decile savings: $210.

## The seat-assignment problem is still here

We covered this in more detail last week, but it matters for the resale math, so quickly: FIFA's own resale listings don't show you the specific section and row until after purchase in many cases. They show you the category. They show you a color-coded zone on the stadium map. You find out the actual seat when FIFA emails you the assignment, which can be weeks after you pay.

StubHub and SeatGeek show you the exact section and row before you click buy. For some buyers that information asymmetry is worth the $84 even if FIFA's list price were identical. When FIFA's list price is *higher* on top of the information asymmetry, it's hard to construct a scenario where FIFA is the rational choice for a US match.

## What the fees do to the math

One detail the FIFA-is-official framing tends to obscure: FIFA's resale marketplace charges a service fee on top of the listing price, and that fee stack is not obviously cheaper than StubHub's or SeatGeek's.

On the Brazil match at Hard Rock mentioned above, the FIFA list price was $478. The fees-in total we captured was $612. That's roughly a 28% fee load, which is in the same neighborhood as Ticketmaster's Platinum/Verified Resale fee stack and higher than SeatGeek's typical 17-22% range. StubHub's fees on the same seat that day came to about 22%.

So the "official" part doesn't mean "lower fees." It means FIFA is running a comparable secondary marketplace with comparable fee structures, priced against the highest-demand US venues where buyers have the least patience and the fewest substitutes.

## What to actually do

If you're shopping for a World Cup match right now, the three-step version is:

First, check which country your match is in. US matches: assume StubHub or SeatGeek will be cheaper and verify. Canada and Mexico matches: check FIFA first, but still verify.

Second, compare all three marketplaces side-by-side before you buy. Don't trust the default assumption that "official" equals "cheapest." On 2 out of 3 US matches, the official marketplace is the most expensive way to get in. That's what the data says.

Third, if you're buying on FIFA and the listing doesn't show you the section and row, factor that uncertainty into the price. A blind Category 3 ticket is not the same product as a Section 135 ticket on StubHub with a photo of the view, even if the category and zone match.
