## The 48-hour proof that "which platform is cheaper" is the wrong question

Last Saturday we pulled Warriors Game 2 prices at Chase Center an hour before tip. The 400-level ceiling seat was $74 on Ticketmaster and $68 on SeatGeek, both all-in. SeatGeek wins by six bucks.

Twenty-four hours later we pulled Rockets Game 2 at Toyota Center, same upper-level category, same pre-game window. It came back at $51 on Ticketmaster and $62 on SeatGeek. Ticketmaster wins by eleven.

Same playoff round. Same weekend. Two identical questions — "is Ticketmaster or SeatGeek cheaper?" — and two opposite answers inside 48 hours. If the answer flips between a Saturday in San Francisco and a Sunday in Houston, it is not a question with a single verdict. The only honest answer is "compare both, every time."

That comparison is the whole reason [TicketScan](/compare) exists. But it helps to know *why* the answer keeps flipping — because once you know the fee math and the platform tendencies, you can estimate which listing will win before the comparison even loads.

Here is the 2026 breakdown.

## Ticketmaster's fee stack in 2026

Ticketmaster is the primary-market giant and a resale marketplace on top of that. For a 2026 ticket you are paying some combination of:

- **Service fee:** 15-25% of ticket face value. NBA playoff tickets typically hit 22-25%. Concerts in the $100-$300 range land at 18-22%. Theater and family shows run 15-17%.
- **Facility charge:** $2-$6 per ticket, set by the venue. MSG, SoFi, and Chase Center are at the top of the range.
- **Order processing fee:** $2-$6 per order (flat, per-order — disproportionately hits small-order buyers).
- **Delivery fee:** Usually zero for mobile transfer.

Stack it all: a listed $100 Ticketmaster ticket is typically $127-$135 at checkout. On events with Platinum dynamic pricing active, the listed price itself is already inflated before fees, so the all-in number is even further from what the venue sold that seat for.

## SeatGeek's fee stack in 2026

SeatGeek is primarily a resale aggregator with a growing primary-market footprint (Cowboys, Patriots, Nets, and roughly a dozen other teams and venues). Its stack:

- **Service fee:** 10-20% of ticket price. Most buyers see 12-16%.
- **No separate facility charge line item.** SeatGeek rolls the venue cut into the service fee.
- **No order processing fee.** No flat per-order add-on.
- **All-in pricing toggle:** Flip a setting and every listing in search shows the final number. Ticketmaster does not offer this. It matters more than it sounds — comparison across platforms takes seconds when you can see the real number in the first tab.

Stack it all: a listed $100 SeatGeek ticket is typically $112-$120 at checkout. Roughly 10-15 percentage points better than Ticketmaster on the same face-value ticket — *if* the listed prices were identical. They usually are not.

## Why the listed prices are not identical

- **Primary vs. resale.** Ticketmaster is the primary source for most major venues. Seats appear there at face value. They appear on SeatGeek only when a reseller lists them, typically at a markup. For on-sale-day in-demand events, Ticketmaster is structurally cheaper.
- **Dynamic pricing bends the primary market.** Ticketmaster's Platinum seats adjust to live demand, sometimes by hundreds of dollars. When Platinum is active, the primary-market advantage disappears.
- **Reseller pricing differs across platforms.** A broker with 12 seats will list them on three marketplaces at three different prices, optimizing per platform.
- **Inventory overlap is partial.** On some playoff games, 40% of one platform's listings do not exist on the other.

Result: the cheapest all-in price for the same physical seat can be on either platform, and it can flip hour to hour.

## Which platform wins by event type — what the 2026 data shows

We have been capturing price pulls across NBA, NHL, summer tours, and World Cup inventory since the start of April. Every number below is all-in.

### NBA playoffs 2026

Across Round 1 Game 1s and Game 2s this week, SeatGeek was cheaper than Ticketmaster on roughly 54% of comparisons, Ticketmaster was cheaper on 34%, and the rest were within a dollar. Median edge when SeatGeek won: $18. Median edge when Ticketmaster won: $23.

Specific examples this week, all-in, same section:

- Celtics vs Heat upper deck: $134 on SeatGeek, $201 on Ticketmaster. SeatGeek by $67.
- Thunder vs Grizzlies upper bowl at Paycom: $78 on SeatGeek, $129 on StubHub. SeatGeek by $51.
- Rockets G2 400-level: $51 on Ticketmaster, $62 on SeatGeek. Ticketmaster by $11.
- Warriors G2 400-level: $68 on SeatGeek, $74 on Ticketmaster. SeatGeek by $6.

The pattern: SeatGeek tends to win upper-bowl and ceiling-tier seats where the service-fee spread matters most. Ticketmaster tends to win when it has primary inventory that has not triggered Platinum yet.

### NHL playoffs 2026

Different story. NHL inventory is thinner on SeatGeek because its partnerships skew NBA and NFL. Ticketmaster was cheaper on about 48% of NHL Round 1 comparisons, SeatGeek on 37%.

Stars vs Wild Game 1 at American Airlines Center, Section 107: $93 on SeatGeek, $118 on Ticketmaster, $151 on StubHub. Panthers G2 at Amerant Bank Arena, Section 124: $127 on SeatGeek, $141 on Ticketmaster.

Rule: check Ticketmaster first when it has primary inventory for the home rink, SeatGeek when it does not.

### Summer concerts 2026

The concert story is the loudest spread in the data. Summer tours (Billie Eilish, Kendrick, Chappell Roan, Tyler the Creator, Sabrina Carpenter) open in June, putting today at the 6-to-8-week pre-show window where inventory is deepest and platforms have not converged.

Billie Eilish at Chase Center, 200-level: $147 on SeatGeek, $212 on Ticketmaster. A $65 spread on the same physical section.

Sabrina Carpenter at MSG, lower bowl: $189 on SeatGeek vs $272 on Ticketmaster. An $83 spread.

Pollstar's Q1 2026 report put the average concert ticket at $108.63 — a five-year high. At that average, a 40% platform spread is real money. The window closes about two weeks pre-show, after which Ticketmaster Platinum usually converges down toward the SeatGeek number rather than up.

If you are buying summer tour tickets, the 6-to-8-week window is the right time and SeatGeek is the right *first* check.

### World Cup 2026

World Cup pricing is its own ecosystem because of the FIFA resale marketplace, which we [covered yesterday](/blog/fifa-resale-three-weeks-data-world-cup-2026). Short version: across 42 group-stage matches, FIFA's own resale was more expensive than the cheaper of StubHub and SeatGeek on 28 of them (66.6%). Median spread: $89 per ticket. Ticketmaster is not a meaningful World Cup platform — the FIFA/StubHub/SeatGeek triangle is where the comparison lives.

For World Cup: SeatGeek or StubHub first, FIFA last for US-venue matches. Flip that order for Azteca, Akron, BBVA, BC Place, or BMO Field.

## When Ticketmaster is worth paying more for

Not often, but sometimes:

- **Buyer protection on primary tickets.** A Ticketmaster primary ticket is guaranteed by the venue. If the show cancels, refund flow is automatic.
- **Last-minute mobile transfer reliability.** Most major venues partner with Ticketmaster for gate scanning. A primary ticket 30 minutes pre-show transfers instantly; resale transfers sometimes lag.
- **Platinum *down-pricing*.** It does not happen often, but Platinum can go below face value for an under-selling event. Ticketmaster is the only platform that will undercut its own listed face on the primary market.

## A quick note on SeatGeek's Deal Score

Deal Score is a within-platform sort, not a cross-platform verdict. It usefully ranks value among SeatGeek's own listings for dense events (6,000-listing concerts). It misleads on thin inventory — a World Cup group-stage match can have a "9/10" label on a seat still $200 over the StubHub price. Treat it as a sort, not a verdict.

## What the fee math actually means

Across 3,400 all-in comparisons in the last four weeks — NBA and NHL playoffs, summer tours, MLB regular season, World Cup — the median savings from picking the cheaper of Ticketmaster or SeatGeek was **$34 per ticket**. The mean was $41. The top-decile savings (the 10% of events with the widest spread) was over $180.

For two seats to one event, that is a round of beers. For a family of four on a summer concert, it is dinner. At the top decile — World Cup and playoff ceiling tickets — it is a night in a hotel.
