## Where we actually are with World Cup 2026 tickets

Today is April 29. The opener is June 11. That gives you 43 days. If you're still trying to get into a match, here is the honest state of the world.

FIFA's lottery phase closed weeks ago. The Last-Minute Sales Phase has been running since April 1, and the official resale marketplace has been running since April 2. Those are the two ways to buy a face-value ticket from FIFA right now. Both have been open for almost four weeks. Both run on dynamic pricing. And both are about to stop being the cheapest place to look for a lot of these matches, if they aren't already.

That last sentence is the one that matters. Here is the math behind it.

## "General sale" doesn't mean what it sounds like

When FIFA says "general sale," what most fans hear is "I can finally walk up and buy a ticket at face value." What it means in 2026 is "you can buy from FIFA's website at whatever price the algorithm thinks the market will pay this hour." The Category 1 final ticket that hit $10,990 in early April is the headline example, but the same engine is running on every match. Every category. Every host country.

The Last-Minute Sales Phase is first-come, first-served, running until each match plays. What's changing fast is which matches still have FIFA-direct inventory at category face value, and which have effectively transitioned to resale-only. Once a category sells through, FIFA's site flips that match into resale mode, and the price you see is whatever a reseller listed. At that point "general sale" quietly becomes "secondary marketplace" without much in the way of an announcement.

We've been pricing the same group-stage matches across FIFA resale, StubHub, and SeatGeek for 27 days now, three times a day, fees-in. Here is what that data is saying as of this morning.

## The 65.5% number

Out of 58 group-stage matches we have enough comparable data on, FIFA's official resale was the most expensive option on 38 of them. That is 65.5%. Two weeks ago, on a smaller dataset, the number was 66.7%. It hasn't really moved.

The median spread between FIFA and the cheapest of the other two platforms, on the matches where FIFA loses: $84 per ticket. Top-decile spread: $210. The single biggest gap we've documented: a Brazil group-stage match at Hard Rock Stadium where FIFA was listing Category 3 at $612 fees-in while StubHub had a comparable seat at $331. That is a $281 difference. Same view, same day, same hour.

If you bought face value in any of those 38 matches, you paid more than you needed to. We covered the methodology and the seat-assignment problem in [our 4/22 piece on three weeks of FIFA resale data](https://www.ticketscan.io/blog/fifa-resale-three-weeks-data-world-cup-2026), if you want the long version. The short version is the number above.

## Where FIFA still wins

Six of the 58 matches have FIFA as the cheapest option, and they cluster in a specific way. Three are at Mexican host venues: Estadio Azteca in Mexico City, Estadio Akron in Guadalajara, Estadio BBVA in Monterrey. Two are at BC Place in Vancouver. One is at BMO Field in Toronto.

The reason is supply. StubHub and SeatGeek are mostly US-focused platforms. Their resale inventory in Mexico and Canada is thin enough that prices on those platforms drift up toward FIFA's listing rather than undercut it. So the practical rule is: if your match is in Mexico City or Vancouver, check FIFA first. If it's in Kansas City or Philadelphia or Atlanta, check FIFA last.

Country first, platform second. That is the rule for the next 43 days.

## What the inventory looks like right now

A lot of the prime US group-stage seats at the marquee venues are no longer available at face value on FIFA. Category 1 and 2 at SoFi, MetLife, and AT&T sold through quickly during the Last-Minute Sales Phase, and what's listed there now is resale priced against the dynamic ceiling. Category 3 and 4 still have face-value pockets at the second-tier US venues — Lincoln Financial in Philadelphia, Arrowhead in Kansas City, NRG in Houston, Gillette in Foxborough — but those pockets are shrinking by the day, and the prices that remain are not what they were when the lottery closed.

Knockout rounds are a different conversation. Round-of-32 and Round-of-16 matches at most US host venues still have FIFA-direct inventory at face. The quarterfinals and semifinals do not, in any meaningful sense. The Final at MetLife is priced where FIFA wants it priced. None of that should surprise anyone, but it is worth saying out loud.

If you've been holding out for a Round-of-32 ticket in a non-glamour US host city, that is still the cheapest seat in the tournament right now. Better than it was at lottery, no. Better than the resale ceiling on the same match in two weeks, almost certainly yes.

## What to do this week

Three steps for the next seven days, in order.

First, decide which match. If you're still flexible — any city, any tier, any opponent — the cheapest path into a World Cup match in 2026 is a non-marquee group-stage game in a smaller US host city, Category 3 or 4. The Final and the opener are not within reach unless your budget is in five figures. A good Round-of-32 match in a smaller US host city is still a real-money ticket, not a fantasy.

Second, run all three platforms before you click buy on any of them. The default move most fans make is to start at FIFA because it's "official" and stop there. The data says that move costs you $84 two times out of three. The cost of pulling up StubHub and SeatGeek alongside FIFA is roughly 90 seconds. The expected savings is at least an order of magnitude more than the time you spend.

Third, set a price alert on the match you want and walk away. Resale prices on US matches will move between now and June 11. The brokers who priced for "average attendee" capitulate as June gets closer, and the dynamic pricing engine recalibrates as section sell-through climbs. Most matches outside the Final and the opener have at least one meaningful dip ahead of them. The alert is the entire reason most fans end up paying less than the people sitting next to them.

## The bigger story

We started this analysis in early April expecting FIFA's official resale to anchor the market. It hasn't. The pattern looks identical to what we saw on Ticketmaster Platinum, on Verified Resale, and on the Springsteen and Oasis presales: dynamic pricing on the primary side pushes face value up to where the secondary market becomes the cheaper option for actual humans trying to buy.

FIFA's pricing engine isn't broken. It's working as designed. It is designed to extract the maximum dollar from a market with roughly 80x more demand than supply. That's what it's doing.

The fan move is to stop assuming "official" means anything about price, and start treating FIFA like one of three platforms with comparable fee loads and comparable inventory churn. Compare. Set alerts. Wait for the dip.

That's the playbook for the next 43 days.
