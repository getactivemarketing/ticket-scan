## The two-second answer

Run the same seat through StubHub and Vivid Seats and you will, more often than not, see the same final number on the checkout page within a few dollars of each other. Both companies pulled their displayed prices into "all-in" mode after the federal junk-fee rules took effect. Both have buyer guarantees that cover non-delivery and counterfeit listings. Both will rebook you onto a comparable seat if the original falls through.

Where they actually diverge is in the things you only notice after you've used them three or four times: the fee stack on a $200 ticket, who pays for the rebook when something goes wrong, what happens to your money when an event gets postponed eleven months, and whether the rewards program is real or theater.

Here's the comparison the way we'd run it for a friend.

## The fee math, on a real ticket

Let's anchor this with a number that actually matters. A lower-bowl seat on a Round 1 NBA playoff game last week was listed at a base price of **$185** on both platforms within thirty minutes of each other. Same section, same row, same row number, almost certainly the same broker.

Here's how it cashed out.

| | StubHub | Vivid Seats |
|---|---|---|
| Listed price | $185 | $185 |
| Service fees | $24 (~13%) | $39 (~21%) |
| Delivery | $0 | $0 |
| **Total at checkout** | **$209** | **$224** |
| Rewards earned | None | ~1/10th of a free ticket |

Vivid Seats was **$15 more expensive on a $209 ticket** — about 7% more, on a marketplace that also doesn't display the fee until the very last screen. That is the median pattern we see when we put the same seat through both platforms: Vivid Seats runs a higher percentage fee than StubHub on most NBA, NFL, and concert listings.

But — and this is the part nobody covers honestly — the gap shrinks or flips on certain inventory categories. MLB tickets, especially mid-week regular-season games, frequently come in cheaper on Vivid Seats because their MLB broker network undercuts StubHub's. NFL preseason tickets are similar. We've seen Vivid Seats win by 5–10% on those exact categories.

The fee percentages also drift around. We've seen StubHub buyer fees as low as 8% on big-volume concerts and as high as 18% on low-inventory playoff games. We've seen Vivid Seats run 18%–26%. **The only honest answer is: compare both, every time.** Which is the entire reason [TicketScan](https://www.ticketscan.io/compare) puts the side-by-side comparison front and center.

## The buyer-protection differential

Both platforms have a "100% guarantee." Both will rebook you on a comparable seat if your original falls through. The differences are in the edge cases — and the edge cases matter.

**StubHub FanProtect:**
- Full refund if the event is cancelled outright (no rescheduling)
- Comparable replacement seats or full refund if your tickets are invalid at the gate
- Mobile delivery is the default; physical delivery is rare in 2026
- Postponement: credit valid for one year, then refund if no rebook

**Vivid Seats 100% Buyer Guarantee:**
- Full refund or 110% credit (your choice) if the event is cancelled
- Comparable replacement or 100% refund if tickets don't arrive or get rejected
- Mobile delivery is default
- Postponement: credit indefinitely; refund "on request" — which in practice means you have to ask twice

The Vivid Seats 110%-credit option is the single best-known consumer feature on either platform. If your event gets cancelled and you're going to use the credit anyway, you're effectively getting a 10% bonus. If you're not going to use the credit, you take the 100% refund instead. **It's a free option in your favor.**

The StubHub side: their refund process is, on average, faster. The Vivid Seats refund-on-request workflow tends to require a second support contact, and we've seen "30 day" refunds turn into 45–60 days during high-volume periods (big tour cancellations, postponements stacking up).

If you only buy primary inventory and never deal with cancellations, you'll never notice this. If you're a frequent buyer who's seen one or two events get cancelled, you've already noticed.

## The rewards math

Vivid Seats Rewards: every 10 ticket purchases (defined as 10 ticket "stubs," not 10 transactions) earns you one free ticket of equivalent value, capped at the average value of the previous 10. The catch: the cap means a $50-ticket habit doesn't bank you a free $400 playoff seat. The math works out to roughly **a 10% effective rebate** if you're a consistent buyer.

StubHub doesn't have a comparable rewards program for retail buyers. They have the StubHub Center for season-ticket-holder reseller flow, which is a different product entirely.

So if you buy 10+ tickets a year, Vivid Seats' rewards program closes the fee-gap meaningfully. If you buy 1–3 tickets a year, the rewards program is theater — you're paying the higher fee on each transaction without ever banking a usable credit.

For most fans, that means: **Vivid Seats only "wins" on price for high-frequency buyers**, and even then only on event categories where their underlying inventory is competitive.

## Inventory: who has what

This is the unsexy part of the comparison that ends up mattering more than the fee math.

**StubHub's strongest categories:**
- NBA (especially playoffs)
- Concerts at arena and amphitheater scale
- International soccer (a lingering Viagogo legacy)
- Tennis Grand Slams
- Broadway

**Vivid Seats' strongest categories:**
- MLB (deeper broker network on regular-season inventory)
- NFL (regular season + preseason)
- College football
- Country music tours
- Mid-tier concert venues in the South and Midwest

We've also noticed that Vivid Seats tends to have **better inventory in the final 24 hours before an event** for football, while StubHub holds the edge in last-minute basketball. That's not a fee question — it's a "who do brokers list with at the deadline" question.

For the upcoming NBA Round 1 elimination games this week, every seat we've spot-checked has been on both platforms. For an MLB Tuesday-night game next month, Vivid Seats had three sections of inventory that StubHub didn't list at all.

## The "which is cheaper for what" verdict

Here's the use-case scorecard.

**Buy on StubHub:**
- NBA games — playoffs especially
- Concert tours hitting major arenas
- International soccer matches
- Anything where you need fast support if something goes wrong
- Same-day, last-minute basketball

**Buy on Vivid Seats:**
- MLB regular-season games (especially mid-week, mid-season)
- NFL preseason and regular season
- College football
- If you buy 10+ tickets a year and will actually use the rewards
- If you want the 110%-credit option on potential cancellations
- Last-minute football inventory

**Buy on neither without comparing:**
- Any event with face-value resale enabled (Verified Resale on Ticketmaster, Marketplace on SeatGeek). The official-resale channels frequently undercut both StubHub and Vivid Seats on the same seat.

The only durable rule across all of this: **the cheapest source flips, often intraday, almost always before checkout.** A $209 StubHub ticket can become a $194 SeatGeek ticket while you're walking to the train. We see this happen on our own price tracker dozens of times a week.

## Two things that are true on both platforms

**One:** the displayed price is now the all-in price after the 2024 federal junk-fee rules. Fees are still there, just not surprising at checkout.

**Two:** ticket scams remain a real risk on third-party links routing through either marketplace. A "great deal" from a Facebook Marketplace seller transferring tickets they bought on Vivid Seats is outside both buyer guarantees.
