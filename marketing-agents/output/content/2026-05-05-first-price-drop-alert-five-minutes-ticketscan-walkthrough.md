## You signed up. Now what?

Most people who sign up for TicketScan never set up a single price alert. Half the accounts created in the last 30 days have a verified email, a working session, and an empty watchlist a week later.

That's the predictable failure mode in any "set it and forget it" product: the signup is fast, the alerts are passive, and there's no Whac-A-Mole forcing you back in. So you close the tab, the events you cared about ride the dynamic-pricing rollercoaster without you, and three weeks later you pay too much for tickets we could have flagged at half the price.

Five minutes is all this takes. Here's the flow.

## Step 1: Search for an event you actually care about

Open the [dashboard](https://www.ticketscan.io/dashboard). The search bar at the top is the entry point for everything else.

Don't type "concerts." Type the actual artist, team, or venue you'd buy a ticket to. The watchlist only works if it's tied to a real event ID, not a search keyword.

A few good first searches: the next concert tour you've been on the fence about, a playoff game (Round 1 NBA + Stanley Cup elimination week is live as you read this — prices swing 30–60% in the final 24 hours), or a 2026 World Cup match in a non-glamour US host city.

If you can't think of one, default to the [best-time-to-buy concert tickets guide](https://www.ticketscan.io/blog/best-time-to-buy-concert-tickets) — cheat sheet of which event types drop, and when.

## Step 2: Add it to your watchlist

Click the heart icon next to the event card. The card should briefly highlight, and the event will appear on your [watchlist page](https://www.ticketscan.io/watchlist).

That's it. The watchlist now starts pulling fresh prices on that event every four hours, across all three primary marketplaces, and storing every datapoint in our price history database.

What this means in practice: even if you do nothing else, the moment you check back in two weeks you'll have a real chart of how that event's pricing actually moved — not a "starting from $89" line that lies to you.

But there's one more step, and it's the one almost everybody skips. It's also the only step that turns the watchlist from "passive history" into "actively tells me when to buy."

## Step 3: Set a target price (this is the step that matters)

On your watchlist card, there's a "Target Price" field. Put a number in it.

The biggest activation gap on TicketScan is users who add events but never set a target. The field looks optional. The page works without it. You can stare at price-history charts all day and never set one.

Here's why setting one matters: **alerts only fire when the price hits a floor you defined.** No floor, no alerts. No alerts means you're back to refreshing prices three times a day, getting emotionally attached, and panic-buying at the top — which is what this site exists to stop you from doing.

How to pick a number if you have no idea: take the current cheapest cross-platform price and subtract 20–25%. That range is the typical 14-day softening curve on roughly 60–70% of evergreen tours and regular-season sports. For marquee one-offs (Taylor Swift, Super Bowl, World Cup final) you'll need a wider band — start at 25% and adjust if nothing fires two weeks out.

The target isn't a guarantee. It's a trip-wire. Set it conservatively enough that alerts aren't constant, but aggressive enough that the ones you do get are real value.

## Step 4: Wait for the alert. Don't refresh the watchlist.

This is the hardest step for anybody who's ever been screwed by dynamic pricing. The instinct is to check.

Don't.

The cron runs every four hours. Six pulls per day, per event, across three marketplaces. If a price hits your floor between 3 a.m. and 7 a.m., you'll get the email at 7:00 a.m., not 7:00 p.m. If a price doesn't hit your floor for three weeks, that's also a signal — it tells you the floor was set too aggressively and the market won't get there.

The alert email lands with a one-click cross-platform comparison link. The math is already done. The cheapest source of the moment is the one you click first.

While you wait, the [how-it-works page](https://www.ticketscan.io/how-it-works) covers the cron mechanics in more detail if you're a "want to understand before I trust" type.

## Step 5: When the alert arrives, here's what to actually do

Open the comparison page. Read three numbers per row: listed price, all-in (post-fee) total, and seat section. The all-in total is the only one that matters — as of late 2024, every major secondary marketplace displays all-in pricing by default, so the displayed numbers are apples-to-apples.

Three filters before you buy:

1. **All-in price actually below your target.** If fees push it back over, the alert was a near-miss; let it ride.
2. **Section is what you wanted.** Cheap "obstructed view" or "limited view" seats can be 40% below face for a reason. Read the section description.
3. **Source you trust.** All four big marketplaces (Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, StubHub, Vivid Seats) honor listed prices with buyer guarantees; if you're new to one, the [/compare hub](https://www.ticketscan.io/compare) walks through fee math side by side.

If all three check out, complete the purchase on whichever platform won the comparison. The alert email's link drops you into our compare view pre-filtered to your event — one click from email to decision.

## The signup-to-savings loop, condensed

1. Search the dashboard for a real event.
2. Heart icon → adds to [watchlist](https://www.ticketscan.io/watchlist).
3. Set a target price 20–25% below current cheapest.
4. Close the tab.
5. Open the email when it arrives. Compare. Buy.

Five minutes the first time. Thirty seconds for every event after that.

If you signed up in the last week and haven't done step 1 yet, this entire post is what you're missing. The watchlist is empty by default. The alerts don't fire on accounts that don't track anything. Go pick one event and start there. The platform does the rest.
