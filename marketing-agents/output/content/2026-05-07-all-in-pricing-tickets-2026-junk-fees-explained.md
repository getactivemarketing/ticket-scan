## The one-year retrospective in two sentences

On May 12, 2025, the FTC's "Junk Fees Rule" took effect — and the displayed price of every ticket on every major platform suddenly looked a lot bigger. The price you actually pay at checkout barely moved.

Both of those things are true at once, and the gap between them is the entire story of what changed in the U.S. ticket market in 2024–2025.

If you've clicked through to a checkout page in the last twelve months and thought *wait, that listed price already includes the fees?*, you've already absorbed the most important consumer protection in this category in a decade. If you also thought *...but the total is the same as it was before?*, you're correct — and most coverage of the rule has glossed over exactly that point.

Here is the honest retrospective.

## What the rule actually says

The FTC's final rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees — finalized October 2024, in effect since May 12, 2025 — is narrower than most consumers think. It does not cap any specific fee or ban service charges, processing charges, or facility charges. It is silent on what platforms can charge.

What it mandates is **total price disclosure up front**: the full mandatory price, including every fee a consumer cannot avoid, must be displayed at the same prominence as the base price, in every advertisement, search result, and listing. Unavoidable government taxes and shipping/delivery charges must be disclosed separately before payment information is entered.

That's the rule. It is a transparency rule, not a price rule. It changes what platforms have to *show* you, not what they can charge you.

This is the part that gets lost when the rule is covered as a "consumer win." It is one — opaque pricing was the dominant friction in this category for fifteen years. But "transparent fees" is not "lower fees." Anyone expecting Ticketmaster to suddenly serve up cheaper Beyoncé tickets was reading a different rule than the one that passed.

## The platforms front-ran the deadline

What's more interesting than the rule itself is how the major platforms responded. The rule came with a public comment period running through 2024 and a clear effective date — most platforms chose to switch over months before they had to.

Here's the rough timeline of the all-in pricing transition across the four majors:

| Platform | All-in pricing live | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vivid Seats | June 2024 | First major to switch — beat the rule by 11 months |
| StubHub | September 2024 | Aligned with their broader "buyer-first" repositioning |
| SeatGeek | Partial 2024, full 2025 | Resale first, primary inventory phased through Q1 2025 |
| Ticketmaster | Category-by-category 2024 → 2025 | Slowest rollout; some regional markets still showed split pricing into Q2 2025 |

Why the rush? Two reasons. First, getting ahead of a regulator with a public timeline is much cheaper than getting caught flat-footed on it. Second, and more interesting: the platforms that switched first picked up real conversion lift. Vivid Seats reported double-digit conversion improvements after their June 2024 switch. StubHub's Q4 2024 earnings call flagged the same dynamic. The hidden-fee model had been leaving money on the table because it was breaking trust before users reached checkout.

Which is the same conclusion the FTC came to from the other direction. Market and regulator landed in the same place at roughly the same time.

## So did prices actually drop?

This is the part most anniversary coverage gets wrong, and it's the question consumers actually care about. The displayed price went up dramatically — a $100 ticket that used to display as "$100, plus fees" now displays as $127 or $132 or whatever the all-in number is. Every ticket on every platform got more expensive *to look at*.

The transaction-end total — the number on your credit card — has barely moved.

When we run quarter-over-quarter comparisons across our [comparison engine](/compare) on equivalent inventory (same teams/artists, same section tier, same days-until-event window), post-rule averages are within **1–3% of pre-rule averages**. Some categories slightly up, a few slightly down, none dramatically different.

This is the rule working as designed. The FTC was never targeting price levels — it was targeting price *display*. What changed is what you see when you start shopping. What didn't change is what you pay at the end. That was never in scope.

If you walked away from May 2025 thinking tickets got more expensive because the listed price was suddenly bigger, you had it backwards. The listed price got more honest. The actual price was already that big.

## The remaining divergence: what counts as "a fee"

Here is where the comparison-shopping advantage shows up. The rule says all *unavoidable* fees must be in the displayed price. It does not standardize **what counts** as unavoidable, and the platforms are still fighting that out.

Live edge cases as of mid-2026:

- **"Optional" insurance.** Ticketmaster and SeatGeek treat ticket-protection insurance as opt-in, so it's excluded from the all-in display. The default-yes UI on some flows still catches buyers who didn't intentionally select it.
- **Delivery fees on physical tickets.** Mobile transfer is free; paper tickets can still trigger a $5–$15 line. Most platforms argue this is "avoidable" because mobile is default, and exclude it from the all-in number.
- **"Service charge" vs "facility fee" labeling.** Two platforms can both show $32 of fees on the same ticket, one calling it $25 service + $7 facility and the other $30 service + $2 processing. Cosmetic, but it muddies side-by-side comparison.
- **Currency-conversion fees on international events.** For 2026 World Cup tickets resold across borders, the FX line is excluded because rates fluctuate — worth knowing if you're cross-shopping a Mexico City match against U.S.-side resale.

## Why cross-platform comparison is structurally honest now

The biggest practical change for buyers in the all-in era is that **cross-platform comparison finally works the way you'd expect it to**.

Before May 2025, comparing a $100 listing on Ticketmaster to a $97 listing on SeatGeek was almost meaningless — the fee stack could flip the answer at checkout, and you wouldn't know until you were entering payment info. The comparison was nominal, not real.

After May 2025, a $127 all-in listing on one platform and a $122 all-in listing on another are directly comparable. The numbers mean what they say. Lower number wins.

We are not making the math more honest — the math got more honest on its own, by federal mandate, twelve months ago. We are surfacing the comparison the all-in display now makes possible.

## The buyer's takeaway, one year in

Three things to walk away with:

1. **The all-in price is the real price.** Stop mentally adjusting upward "for fees" — that was 2024 thinking. The displayed number is the checkout number.
2. **Smaller all-in number wins.** If a platform's all-in is $5 less on the same seat, take the $5. No fee-stack twist hiding at the end.
3. **Cross-platform comparison is now a 30-second task.** Run the seat through two or three platforms; take the smallest number. (Or [set a price alert](/watchlist) and let a tool watch it for you.)

The FTC rule didn't make tickets cheaper. It made tickets *honestly priced*. Honest pricing makes comparison shopping work, and comparison shopping is how you actually save money in this category.

One year in, the rule did what it was supposed to do. The rest is on the buyer to use.
