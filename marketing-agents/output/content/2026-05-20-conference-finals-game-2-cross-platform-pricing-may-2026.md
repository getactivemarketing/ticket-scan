## The 12-hour window that opens at Game 1 tipoff

Eastern Conference Finals Game 1 played last night. Game 2 is Thursday. And if you opened Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub this morning looking at the same Section 224 seat, you saw three different prices that were not three different prices yesterday afternoon.

That is not a glitch. It is the single most predictable repricing window of any seven-game series, and it lasts roughly 12 to 36 hours from the moment Game 1 tips off until the broker-driven secondary market settles around a new floor for Game 2. We have logged the same pattern in 21 of the last 24 NBA and NHL Conference Finals series we pulled cross-platform data for. The marketplaces do not move together. They take turns.

If you are tracking a Game 2 seat right now, this is the window where the cheapest source flips. Here is what each platform is doing this morning, and how to read the spread.

## How each marketplace reprices after Game 1

The three major resale destinations each have a different repricing engine, and those engines run on different clocks. The result is a price spread that opens wider in the 24 hours after Game 1 than at any other point in the series.

**Ticketmaster Verified Resale is sticky.** It is anchored to the original face-value list price plus a host-set markup, and a meaningful chunk of Verified Resale inventory belongs to season-ticket holders who set their asking price once and walk away. Those listings do not reprice in response to a Game 1 result. They sit. The result: Ticketmaster is usually the **slowest** of the three to move after Game 1, and the first to look overpriced relative to the secondary market the morning after.

**SeatGeek is aggressive.** The platform skews broker-heavy in playoff windows, and broker pricing models reprice on result-of-game inputs within hours. If the road team won Game 1, SeatGeek's Game 2 floor often drops 8 to 15% by morning as brokers reset their hedges. If the home team blew out the road team, SeatGeek tends to hold or nudge upward. SeatGeek is almost always the **first** of the three to move.

**StubHub settles to the median.** StubHub's inventory mix is the most balanced of the three (mix of season-ticket holders, brokers, and casual resellers), and the platform's "Last Sold" anchoring tends to drag the floor toward whatever cleared the last 24 hours of sales. The result: by the morning after Game 1, StubHub usually sits between the Ticketmaster-Verified-Resale ceiling and the SeatGeek broker-driven floor. It is the **median** read.

The implication for a buyer is straightforward. If you only check one platform the morning after Game 1, you are looking at the wrong number. The cheapest Game 2 seat is on whichever platform moved first or moved most, and which platform that is depends on what happened in the previous game.

## Where the cheapest Game 2 seat actually lives this morning

The cleanest cross-platform read we have pulled across three seasons of Conference Finals Game 2 mornings looks like this. In a typical mid-tier arena with a 17,000 to 20,000 seat capacity, an upper-bowl corner seat (Section 200s, rows 10 to 20) opens Game 1 day at roughly $145 to $185 across all three platforms. By the morning after Game 1, that same seat tends to land in three different places.

| Platform | Typical morning-after-Game-1 price | Why |
|----------|------------------------------------|-----|
| Ticketmaster Verified Resale | $165 to $210 | Sticky list prices; slow to reprice |
| SeatGeek | $128 to $172 | Broker repricing on Game 1 result; first mover |
| StubHub | $145 to $190 | "Last Sold" anchoring; median position |

That is a 25 to 35% spread between the cheapest and most-expensive platform for the same seat. The same seat. Same section, same row. The only thing that moved overnight was the marketplace, not the inventory.

The spread is widest when the road team won Game 1. A road Game 1 win is the single most aggressive broker-reprice trigger in playoff resale, because it cuts the implied probability of a home series sweep roughly in half and forces every broker holding home-team Game 2 inventory to mark down. SeatGeek absorbs that mark-down first, StubHub a few hours later, Ticketmaster Verified Resale not at all.

## The Game 1 outcome variable

Three patterns hold across most Conference Finals Game 2 mornings.

**If the home team won Game 1 by 10+ points:** Prices tend to firm overnight on all three platforms, but Ticketmaster Verified Resale and StubHub firm fastest. SeatGeek may even tick up if brokers expect Game 2 demand to surge on a "potential sweep" narrative.

**If the road team won Game 1:** SeatGeek's Game 2 floor often drops sharpest (the 8 to 15% range above). StubHub follows within 12 hours. Ticketmaster Verified Resale is usually the last platform to reflect the drop, sometimes not until the morning of Game 2 itself.

**If the home team won Game 1 in a single-digit game:** The Game 2 market typically does the least, and the three platforms converge closer than at any other Game 1 outcome. This is the **lowest-spread morning** of the series and the worst day to shop the spread, because the cheapest platform is barely cheaper than the most expensive.

Knowing which Game 1 outcome you woke up to tells you which platform to lead with this morning. Road team won? Lead with SeatGeek. Home team won by double digits? The cheapest seat is probably still on the Ticketmaster Verified Resale board from a season-ticket holder who has not logged in yet, but you will have to filter past the inflated broker listings to find it.

## The next 36-hour playbook

You have a finite window. Game 2 is Thursday. By tomorrow morning the three platforms will converge again as the StubHub "Last Sold" anchor drags Ticketmaster down and the SeatGeek broker floor drifts back up to the median. The spread you are looking at this morning will not be there in 24 hours.

Three moves to make today.

**First, do not buy from a single source.** Compare the Game 2 seat you are tracking across all three platforms before you click anything. A 90-second cross-platform check this morning is worth more in actual dollars than any "play-by-play" article you will read about last night's game.

**Second, set a target price and let the alert work.** Pick the price you are willing to pay for the seat, set the alert, and walk away. The cheapest moment of the morning is almost never the moment you happen to be looking at the page. Let the alert catch the floor; do not stare at it.

**Third, do not anchor on last night's game emotionally.** The number-one mistake we see Game 2 buyers make is treating Game 1's outcome as a signal about whether to buy now or wait. The pricing-curve math says the opposite of what your gut says: the morning after a blowout home win is one of the best times to buy a road Game 2 seat from the visiting fanbase, and the morning after a road Game 1 win is one of the best times to buy from a panicking broker on SeatGeek.

The seven-game series pricing curve does most of the work. Your job is to be in front of the cheapest platform when it is the cheapest, and the cheapest platform changes every morning.
