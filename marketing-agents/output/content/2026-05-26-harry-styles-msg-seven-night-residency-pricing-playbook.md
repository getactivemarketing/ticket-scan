Harry Styles is doing seven nights at Madison Square Garden between August 26 and September 9. That is the entire ask. No co-headliner, no touring opener, no supporting cast — just seven Harry Styles shows at one building inside a fifteen-day window. Wednesday, Friday, Saturday, then a week off, then Wednesday, Friday, Saturday, then four days off, then a final Wednesday closer.

If you are trying to go to one of them, the question is not whether to buy. It is which night.

This is the part most fans get wrong. The instinct is to grab the night that fits your calendar and hit "buy" the moment Verified Resale opens. The instinct is expensive. A seven-night residency at the same venue, with the same production, by the same artist, on the same tour does not produce seven equivalently-priced shows. It produces a pricing curve. And the curve is predictable enough that you can save anywhere from 20% to 45% on the same physical seat by picking the right night.

Here is what the curve looks like, why it bends the way it does, and where the bargain seats actually live.

## Why a seven-night residency is not seven shows

A few weeks ago we published a longer piece on the rise of three-night residencies as the default 2026 stadium-tour booking shape. The short version: when an artist plays the same venue multiple nights in a row, the first night is the marquee, the second night softens 15-25%, and the third night's pricing is decided almost entirely by the day of the week it lands on.

Extend that framework to a seven-night arc and three things happen.

First, the marquee premium stays concentrated on Night 1. Aug 26 is the *tour residency opener* — the one the music press writes about, the one social media livestreams, the one every Harry Styles fan in the country wishes they were at. That night runs hot.

Second, the weekend nights inside the residency build their own pricing cluster independent of opening-night premium. Saturday Aug 29 is Night 3 by sequence, but it is also *opening-weekend Saturday at MSG*, and that second label carries its own ceiling. Same goes for Saturday Sept 5, though by then the residency-fatigue effect kicks in.

Third — and this is the part the casual buyer never figures out — the back-half weeknights are where the floor lives. Wednesday Sept 9 is Night 7. It is mid-week. It is post-Labor-Day. The casual fan who flew in for Aug 28-29 weekend has been home for a week and a half. The international fan who flew in for Sept 4-5 weekend just flew out four days ago. Anyone left for the Sept 9 closer is local, flexible, and willing to wait. Resellers know that. Their listing prices reflect it.

## The seven-night pricing map

| Night | Date | Day | Position on the curve | Target price vs Night 1 |
|-------|------|-----|----------------------|------------------------|
| 1 | Aug 26 | Wed | Tour-residency opener (marquee premium, structural ceiling) | 1.00x |
| 2 | Aug 28 | Fri | Opening Friday (high but softening) | ~0.85x |
| 3 | Aug 29 | Sat | Opening Saturday (weekend ceiling, dual-premium night) | ~0.95x |
| 4 | Sept 2 | Wed | Mid-residency weeknight (first soft window) | ~0.65x |
| 5 | Sept 4 | Fri | Closing-weekend Friday (modest weekend lift) | ~0.75x |
| 6 | Sept 5 | Sat | Closing Saturday (weekend, but residency-fatigue starting) | ~0.80x |
| 7 | Sept 9 | Wed | Closer + mid-week + post-Labor-Day (structural floor) | ~0.55x |

The 0.55x at Night 7 is not a guess. It is the framework. A pure mid-week non-marquee closing night of a multi-week residency at a venue with this much weekday supply gets discounted hard on the secondary market by the close of the residency window. The exact percentage will move with the seat tier — premium floor seats compress less than upper-bowl resales, where the discount can run wider. But the directional ranking of cheapest-to-priciest holds across every seat tier in the building.

If you have a hard date constraint — you can only do Saturday because of your shift schedule, your kid's school, whatever — pick Saturday and stop reading. The map is for fans who can flex.

### The buyer's playbook

If you can flex, this is the move:

**Target Wednesday Sept 9 first.** Use the floor-pricing logic. Run a price-comparison sweep across Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub at least three times a week starting the day after Verified Resale opens. The Sept 9 lows will compound. By the second week of August, you should be seeing a 30-45% discount on equivalent seats vs Aug 26.

**Hedge with Wednesday Sept 2 if Sept 9 is sold to your tier early.** Sept 2 is the second-cheapest night by the framework. The risk: if you wait too long for Sept 9 and your seat tier sells out, Sept 2 may have moved up in the meantime. Run alerts on both Wednesdays in parallel.

**Skip Aug 29 unless your fandom outvotes your budget.** Saturday Aug 29 is the most-expensive ticket you can buy for this residency. The Apple Music livestream night already happened on Wednesday. The weekend marquee night already happened on Friday. You are paying full weekend-opener premium for the third-best night of the run.

**Set price alerts, not auto-buys.** This is the playbook we have written about more than anything else, and it matters more here than for almost any other event. Across seven nights at MSG with three platforms running dynamic pricing, you are looking at twenty-one separately-moving price points. No human is going to monitor that manually without losing their mind. Set a target price per night, walk away, and let the alert system find your number.

## The lazy version

If all of this is too much and you just want a ticket, here is the single best decision: run a watchlist sweep on all seven dates at once with the same target price across the board, then take whichever night clears your target first. Most buyers who do this end up with Sept 9, Sept 2, or Aug 28 — in roughly that order. The unlucky ones end up with Aug 29 because they could not wait. The lucky ones end up with Sept 9 because they did.

Either way: do not pay opening-night money for closing-night supply.
