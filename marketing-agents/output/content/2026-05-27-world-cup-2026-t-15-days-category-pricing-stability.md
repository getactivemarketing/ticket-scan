## Fifteen days to kickoff, and the pricing engine is doing something weird

World Cup 2026 opens at SoFi Stadium on June 11. That gives you exactly fifteen days from today to figure out whether to buy now, wait, or walk away. Most fans treating this like a normal "last-minute ticket" decision are about to make an expensive mistake — because the FIFA pricing engine is not behaving like a normal last-minute pricing engine.

Here is the pattern that has been quietly emerging in the data since the Last-Minute Sales Phase opened on April 1. The cheap seats are not moving. The expensive seats are. And that is the opposite of how a healthy primary market is supposed to work.

We covered the venue side of this story on Monday — the three US host stadiums (Levi's, Gillette, Lincoln Financial) where every match is a Group Stage match and the Category 4 floor lives at $80, $110, and $125. Today is the category side of the same story. If you understand it, the buyer's playbook for the next fifteen days writes itself.

## The four FIFA categories are behaving like four different markets

FIFA sells World Cup tickets in four pricing categories per match. Category 1 is the premium midfield-level inventory. Category 2 wraps the corner and end zones at the same elevation. Category 3 sits one level up. Category 4 is the upper bowl behind the goals — the structural floor of the building.

Across the eight weeks since April 1, here is what each category has actually done.

| Category | Typical Group-Stage Range | Behavior Since April 1 | Action at T-15 |
|----------|---------------------------|------------------------|----------------|
| Cat 1 (premium midfield) | $850 – $2,985+ (opener) | Steady upward repricing, 12–22% lift | Hold, watch resale |
| Cat 2 (corners, same level) | $450 – $1,400 | Volatile, both directions | Hold, watch resale |
| Cat 3 (mid-level) | $185 – $475 | Mostly flat, light upward drift | Buy if it fits |
| Cat 4 (upper bowl) | $80 – $250 | Flat inside a ~5% band | Buy or take what's left |

Three of those rows tell a coherent story. Cat 4 inventory is sticky at face value because FIFA priced the floor where it wanted the headline number to land ("World Cup tickets from $60") and the engine has no incentive to drop below it. Cat 3 has light upward drift because that is the band where casual buyers default — middle of the bowl, middle of the price range, "good enough" seats. Cat 1 has steady upward repricing because that is the band the dynamic engine was actually built to optimize. The premium midfield seats are where every dollar of demand-pull gets captured.

The interesting row is Cat 2. Cat 2 is volatile both directions. That is the band where the secondary market is actively undercutting FIFA-direct on roughly half the matches we have tracked. Real humans bought Cat 2 inventory in the lottery phase, decided they would rather not fly to Houston in June, and listed it on StubHub or SeatGeek at whatever price closes the sale. Meanwhile FIFA's own resale platform is competing for the same seats — but FIFA's engine is repricing toward the dynamic ceiling. Result: same row, same row number, sometimes a $200 spread between FIFA-direct and the cheapest resale listing.

### The cross-platform variance frame at T-15

This is where TicketScan does the work nobody wants to do manually. Pulling up any single match and looking at Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub side-by-side will show you the spread in real time. For Cat 4 matches the spread is small (because nobody is dumping $80 floor seats — they sell). For Cat 1-2 matches the spread is the entire story. We have seen $400 deltas on the same Cat 1 row, same match, same week.

The next ten days are the window where Cat 1-2 sellers who are not going to use their seats start hitting "lower price." That dip does not happen in Cat 4. It does not really happen in Cat 3. It happens almost exclusively in Cat 1-2, and almost exclusively on the secondary platforms.

## The T-15 buyer's playbook, by category

Three rules, sorted by which category you are targeting.

**If you are targeting Cat 4 (the cheap seats):** Buy now, or take whatever inventory is left at face value the week of the match. The dip is not coming. Cat 4 inventory thins fast in the final week, and the last few seats are pinned to face value or above. Waiting to "see if it drops" is the exact mistake that produces nosebleed-or-nothing decisions on June 10.

**If you are targeting Cat 3 (the middle band):** Buy if the seat fits your match and your travel plan. The drift is upward, but slowly enough that most buyers will not see meaningful movement either way over the next two weeks. The risk-reward of waiting is roughly neutral. The biggest reason to delay would be that you are still deciding which match to attend — not that you expect a better price.

**If you are targeting Cat 1 or Cat 2 (the premium bands):** Hold. Set a cross-platform price alert. The dynamic engine is going to keep moving the FIFA-direct number toward the ceiling, but the real action is on resale, where humans with non-refundable inventory are about to start the "I need to sell these" phase. A cross-platform watchlist is the only way to catch that without checking three sites four times a day for the next two weeks.

The one thing every category has in common: do not buy on the first platform you check. The $80 Cat 4 floor at Levi's is the same number on Ticketmaster and SeatGeek and StubHub, but the fees are not, and the $1,400 Cat 2 seat at SoFi on Ticketmaster might be $1,180 on SeatGeek for the exact same row. Compare every category, every match, every day.

## The bigger picture at T-15

FIFA's dynamic pricing engine is not built to find the buyer the best deal. It is built to capture the highest dollar the highest-paying buyer will tolerate for each category, and to leave the floor alone because the floor is the marketing headline. That is fine. That is how every modern primary ticketing system works.

What is new is that the resale market has now had eight full weeks to absorb the lottery winners, the secondary buyers, the corporate hospitality reseats, and the speculators. The next fifteen days are the part of the cycle where the resale market does what it does best: clear inventory at the price the buyer is actually willing to pay, not the price the algorithm wants.

If you are still deciding which match to attend, Monday's piece on the three cheapest US venues is the starting point. If you have already picked your match and you are deciding when to buy, the framework above is the answer. It is going to be one of two answers: buy now, or set an alert and wait. There is no third answer in the next fifteen days.
