## Two championships, one week, two completely different markets

The 2026 NBA Finals tip off in roughly seven to ten days. The Stanley Cup Final starts inside the same window. Both series will run concurrently for the next three weeks. Both peak in organic search volume for "ticket prices" in early June. Both will be on every sports bar TV in the country.

That is where the similarity ends.

The pricing math behind the two series is wildly different, and most of the cross-platform comparison work that should be happening this week is not happening anywhere except inside TicketScan's own dataset. Competitor blogs cover each series in isolation. None of them put the two side by side and ask the actual question fans are asking: if I am going to spend money on one Game 1 ticket this June, which series is the better value?

Here is the version of the answer based on the cross-platform data we have been pulling from the last four NBA Finals and the last four Stanley Cup Finals.

## The cross-league comparison framework

The cleanest way to compare two championship series is the per-game pricing band, not the series-total spend. Here is roughly where each league has historically landed at face value for the cheapest available ticket on the day of, plus the historical cross-platform resale floor for the same seat tier.

| Game 1 (home market) | $480 – $850 | $310 – $580 |
| Game 2 (home market) | $390 – $620 | $290 – $510 |

Read across any single row. The Stanley Cup Final is consistently 30 to 45 percent cheaper than the NBA Finals at the cheapest-available tier, every single game. That is not a small spread. That is the difference between a manageable splurge and a "let me look at my credit limit" decision.

### Per-league pricing benchmark, in one sentence each

The NBA Finals is the most expensive championship round in North American sports outside the Super Bowl. The Stanley Cup Final is, dollar for dollar at the cheapest tier, the most accessible major-league championship of the calendar year.

That holds even when the NHL series goes to a smaller US market or a Canadian market. Bell Centre in Montreal, Climate Pledge in Seattle, Honda Center in Anaheim. All of them have lower structural ceilings than the marquee NBA Finals venues simply because the buildings are smaller and the corporate buyer pool is shallower. The NBA Finals lives in Madison Square Garden, Crypto.com Arena, TD Garden, and a handful of other top-tier buildings where the lower bowl is corporate-owned for the entire season. The premium gets baked into Game 1 and never softens.

## The venue-premium math

Two structural reasons the NBA Finals always commands a premium over the Stanley Cup Final at every game stage.

The first is the lower-bowl ratio. NBA arenas seat roughly 18,000 to 21,000. NHL arenas seat roughly 17,000 to 21,000. Almost identical totals. But the share of seats devoted to the lower bowl is not. NBA buildings push more inventory into the lower bowl, and the lower bowl is where corporate buyers concentrate. Hockey upper bowls are bigger as a percentage of total inventory. More upper-bowl seats means more fan inventory eventually hitting resale, which means a softer cross-platform floor over the course of the series.

The second is city-brand overlap. The NBA Finals almost always lands in a city with a marquee NBA brand attached. Lakers, Celtics, Knicks, Warriors. Those brand names alone justify a 20 to 30 percent ticket-price premium even before the playoffs start. The Stanley Cup Final routinely lands in cities without the same retail-brand premium. Florida, Tampa, Vegas, Edmonton. Strong hockey fan bases. Smaller national brand pull.

Add those two factors together, and a Stanley Cup Game 1 in a non-marquee NHL market is roughly the same ticket-value tier as an NBA Conference Semifinals game in a major market. Different round. Same accessibility. The marketing tells you they are not comparable. The data says they are.

### Per-league cross-platform structural advantage

There is one more wrinkle that matters if you are actually buying. The platform that owns the best resale inventory differs by league.

| League | Strongest resale platform at the cheapest tier | Typical cross-platform spread |
|--------|------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| NBA Finals | Ticketmaster Verified Resale | 8 – 15% vs StubHub / SeatGeek |
| Stanley Cup Final | StubHub | 12 – 22% vs Ticketmaster / SeatGeek |

The NBA's tight integration with Ticketmaster means the league funnels its richest resale inventory into Verified Resale. Game 1 lower-bowl seats almost always surface on Ticketmaster first. The same seat is usually 10 percent more on the other major secondary marketplaces — not because those platforms are gouging, but because they have to source the inventory through different channels.

The Stanley Cup Final flips that. NHL resale inventory historically clears strongest on StubHub, where the platform's larger international buyer base actually competes for upper-bowl tickets. SeatGeek and Ticketmaster end up with thinner NHL inventory pools and slightly higher floors as a result.

If you check only one platform this week, you are systematically overpaying. The cheapest seat to the same game on the same row is on a different platform depending on which league you are buying.

## The buyer playbook for both Finals

Three rules. Same logic across both leagues.

Pick the cheapest game on the schedule that you are actually willing to attend. For the NBA Finals, that is Game 2 in the home market (the unsexy answer; also the cheapest answer at the lower-bowl floor). For the Stanley Cup Final, that is Game 3 or Game 4 in the road market, where local hockey demand thins out and casual sellers list early.

Run a cross-platform check before you click "buy" anywhere. The platform that owns the best inventory differs by league. It also differs by game stage. Game 1 is a Ticketmaster Verified Resale moment. Game 5 in a long series is a StubHub-late-clearance moment. Use the tool that pulls all three platforms at once instead of guessing.

Set a price alert and walk away. Both series will see at least one meaningful resale dip per game in the 72 hours before tip-off or puck drop. The fans who get the best deals are the ones who set an alert on Monday and ignore the noise until the price hits.

The 2026 NBA Finals is the more expensive ticket. The 2026 Stanley Cup Final is the better value. If you can only spend on one championship this June, the math is not subtle.
