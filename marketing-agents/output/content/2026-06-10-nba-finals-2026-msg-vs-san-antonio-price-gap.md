## A seat is a seat — until it isn't

It's Knicks–Spurs in the 2026 NBA Finals. New York leads the series 2-1, Game 4 tips tonight at Madison Square Garden, and the same product — one ticket, one game, the same two teams — is selling for wildly different prices depending on which building you walk into.

Here are the numbers, all from this week's press, not our own data:

- A get-in (cheapest available) seat to **Game 4 at MSG**: **$13,159**, per Yahoo Sports and ESPN.
- A get-in seat to the **games in San Antonio**: **under $1,000**, per Yahoo Sports.

That's more than a 10x spread for the literal same series. The cheapest way to watch Knicks–Spurs in the Finals in person isn't a worse seat or a worse game. It's a different zip code.

This is the kind of thing we exist to point at. So let's break down why the gap is this enormous — and what a normal fan can actually do about it.

## Why New York is the most expensive room in sports right now

A few forces are stacking on top of each other at MSG, and none of them have anything to do with the basketball.

### 1. The Knicks haven't been here in a generation

Scarcity of memory is its own kind of demand. A fanbase that has waited decades to host a Finals game does not haggle. When Game 3 hit MSG earlier this week, the cheapest seat was **$3,940** with fees, the average sold ticket was **$7,683**, and the priciest seat still listed sat at **$30,970** — with the highest *sold* seat going for roughly **$65,000** (ESPN). Front Office Sports clocked listings pushing past **$40,000**.

### 2. New York is simply a more expensive resale market

The same seat quality lists higher in New York than almost anywhere else in the country, Finals or not. Corporate buyers, a deep luxury market, and a city full of people willing to pay for a story all sit on top of fixed arena supply (MSG holds about 19,800). More money chasing the same seats means one thing: the floor goes up.

### 3. Demand-based pricing does the rest

Resale prices aren't set by a person picking a fair number. They float on live demand, the same engine behind concert "dynamic pricing" and FIFA's variable World Cup pricing happening this very week. When a market is hot, the model climbs toward whatever someone will pay — and in New York right now, someone will pay a lot.

Even Knicks guard Josh Hart said the quiet part out loud, calling the MSG Finals prices "ridiculous" and lamenting that real fans were getting priced out (Fox News).

## San Antonio: the same Finals, a tenth of the price

The Spurs are the higher seed and own home court (the 2-2-1-1-1 format puts Games 1, 2, and a potential Game 5 and 7 in San Antonio). Game 5 lands there this **Saturday, June 13**. And get-in prices for the San Antonio games have run **under $1,000** — a fraction of the New York number for the exact same matchup.

Why? Smaller resale market, a fanbase used to deep playoff runs, less corporate crush, and demand that simply doesn't sit at New York's altitude. None of that changes what happens on the court. It changes only what it costs to watch it.

## The lesson for every fan, not just this series

The Knicks–Spurs gap is dramatic, but the underlying rule is universal: **the same event is priced very differently across venues, and even within a single game, across resale platforms.** Two things follow.

### Location is a pricing lever fans underrate

If you're flexible on which game to attend, the "where" can matter more than the "when." A road-game seat in a cheaper market plus a flight can land well under a home-market get-in. For a traveling fan, the math is often not close.

### Within any one game, the cheapest source keeps moving

This is the part people skip. For a single seat to a single game, Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub rarely show the same price — and which one is cheapest flips constantly, sometimes intraday, as sellers reprice against each other. Buying on the first platform you open is how you overpay by hundreds without ever knowing it.

## The bottom line

A $13,159 seat and a sub-$1,000 seat to the same Finals tell you everything about how modern ticket pricing actually works: it's not about the game, it's about the room and the moment. The fans who win aren't the ones who spend the most — they're the ones who compare before they buy and stay flexible on where they sit.
