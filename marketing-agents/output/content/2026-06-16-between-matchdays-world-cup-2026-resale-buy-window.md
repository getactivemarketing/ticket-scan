## Resale prices are not flat. They breathe.

If you have been refreshing a listing page for a specific World Cup match, you have probably noticed the number doesn't sit still. It climbs the closer you get to kickoff, then slumps a little once that match is in the books, then starts climbing again as the next fixture nears.

That is not random. Resale pricing tracks attention, and attention tracks the schedule. When a team plays, every casual buyer in two countries is suddenly shopping for that match at once. The day after, those same people stop looking. The listings are still there. The urgency is gone.

This is the window most fans miss. They shop when the match is loud, which is exactly when the price is highest. The cheaper moment is the boring stretch in between, when a team is sitting idle and the resale market goes quiet waiting for the next reason to care.

## Where we are in the calendar right now

The group stage runs June 11 to roughly June 27. The opening round of fixtures wrapped over the weekend. The second round of group games runs Wednesday through Friday this week (June 17–19), with matches like Portugal–DR Congo and England–Croatia on the slate. After that, every group plays its third game, the bracket fills in, and the Round of 32 opens June 28 and runs to July 3.

So there are two soft spots worth watching:

- **The days right after a team's match**, before its next fixture is close enough to spook buyers.
- **The lull after the group stage closes**, when knockout matchups are still settling and a lot of buyers are waiting to see who actually advances before they commit.

That second window is the bigger opportunity, and it is almost here.

## Why the dip is real, not wishful thinking

A few things are pushing prices up hard right now, and all of them fade in the quiet days.

Demand is concentrated. According to Yahoo Sports and an Edmunds analysis of opening-week listings, the five most in-demand matches jumped between 92% and 136% in just three days, and more than half of the 92 remaining matches were carrying a four-figure get-in price. That is what a spike looks like. Spikes pull back. They always do, once the immediate match passes and the panic buyers clear out.

Sellers also reprice on a clock. A listing that sat untouched through a team's idle stretch is a listing the seller starts to sweat. Drop the price, or hold and risk eating the ticket. Plenty of them blink. You just have to be watching when they do.

The catch: you cannot watch every listing on three platforms by hand, all day, for a week. Nobody can. That is the whole reason price alerts exist.

## How to actually catch the dip

Here is the playbook. It takes about five minutes to set up and then runs without you.

### 1. Pick your match and your seat reality

Decide the exact match you want and roughly where you'd sit. "Somewhere in the upper bowl for the New Jersey Round of 32 game" is specific enough. Vague shopping leads to impulse buying, which leads to paying the spike price. Know what you want before the market tries to rush you.

### 2. Name your floor with a little math

Look at the current get-in price across Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub and write down the lowest of the three. Your target should sit under that, in the realm the price actually hit during a recent quiet stretch, not a fantasy number. A target 10% to 15% below today's get-in is aggressive but reachable in an idle window. If you set it at half price, your alert will simply never fire, and you'll end up buying in a panic anyway.

### 4. When it fires, move

A real dip in a hot market does not last. If your alert fires during a team's idle stretch or the post-group-stage lull, that is your window, not an invitation to wait for it to drop another twenty bucks. Greed is how the dip closes before you buy.

## The comparison piece people skip

Even inside a dip, the three platforms rarely agree. They pull from different seller pools and reprice on different schedules, so the same section can be meaningfully cheaper on one site than another at the exact same minute. Buying the first listing you see, even a "good" one, leaves money on the table.

A 30-second three-way comparison before you check out is the difference between catching the dip and catching most of it. When demand is this concentrated, the spreads between platforms tend to get wider, not narrower, which makes that quick check worth more than usual.

## The honest version

None of this guarantees a bargain. The Final on July 19 at MetLife was averaging around $16,849 a ticket on TickPick as of June 15, and no clever timing trick is going to make that a deal. For the marquee matches, the dip is a discount on something expensive, not a path to cheap.

But for plenty of group-stage and early-knockout games, the difference between buying on a matchday spike and buying in the quiet days is real money. The fans who pay the most are almost always the ones who shopped when everyone else was shopping.
