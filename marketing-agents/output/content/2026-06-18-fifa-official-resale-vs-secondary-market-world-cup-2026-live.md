## The question changed the day the tournament started

A week before kickoff, the World Cup ticket question was simple: who has the lowest get-in price right now? We answered it back then, and the honest verdict was "it depends on the match, check both."

Now that matches are actually being played, a different question matters more. You are no longer racing a release window. You are deciding how to buy a specific seat for a specific match, often a few days out, and the deciding factor has quietly become the fees and the mechanics, not the sticker price.

There are two real ways to buy a World Cup 2026 ticket as a regular fan: FIFA's official resale marketplace, or the secondary platforms (StubHub, SeatGeek, Ticketmaster, Vivid Seats). Most people assume official is both safest and cheapest. Only one of those is reliably true.

## The number nobody mentions: FIFA resale charges 30%

Here is the fact that should reframe the whole decision. When you buy a resold ticket through FIFA's own marketplace, FIFA charges a 15% fee to the seller and a 15% fee to the buyer. That is 30% skimmed off every transaction, per worldcuppass.com, ticket-compare.com, and FIFA's own customer support pages.

Read that again, because it cuts against the instinct. "Buy official to avoid getting gouged" sounds right. But on a resold ticket, FIFA's platform takes a 30% cut that lands on the same two people in the deal. The seller eats 15% and prices it back into the listing, and you pay 15% on top.

That does not make FIFA resale a scam. The tickets are guaranteed real, the original is cancelled and reissued straight to your FIFA account, and there is no scalper between you and the seat. You are paying for certainty. Just be clear that the "official" path is the safe one, not automatically the cheap one.

## What the secondary market is doing right now

While that 30% sits baked into FIFA's marketplace, the open secondary market is moving the other way. Resale prices on StubHub and SeatGeek are falling, not climbing.

TicketData.com, the analytics group cited by NBC News, reports prices coming down on more than 90% of matches: roughly 24% lower than a month ago and about 8% lower over the past week. Part of the reason is supply. In late May, around 44,000 tickets vanished from FIFA's official portal and resurfaced on the secondary platforms at prices below FIFA's own site (NBC News; Newsweek; Consequence).

When inventory floods the open market and demand does not keep pace, sellers undercut each other to move seats. That is the dynamic right now for most of the group stage. The marquee matches and host-nation games still hold their price. The neutral fixtures in 70,000-seat NFL stadiums are the ones softening.

## Side by side: how the two stack up mid-tournament

| What you care about | FIFA Official Resale | Secondary (StubHub / SeatGeek) |
|---|---|---|
| Fees | 15% buyer + 15% seller = 30% per transaction | Buyer fee varies by platform, typically below FIFA's 15% buyer cut |
| Counterparty risk | None; FIFA cancels and reissues the ticket digitally | Platform guarantees the sale, but you are buying from a stranger |
| Price direction now | Tracks listed inventory; 30% fee is fixed | Falling on 90%+ of matches per TicketData |
| Inventory depth | Thinner; depends on what FIFA holders relist | Deeper after the late-May inventory shift onto resale |
| Delivery | Mobile only, live QR in the FIFA app near match day | Mobile transfer, often through the same FIFA app |

The takeaway from that table: FIFA resale wins on peace of mind, and the secondary market increasingly wins on price for anything that is not a glamour fixture. For a softening neutral match, the secondary floor minus a smaller buyer fee will often beat the same seat carrying FIFA's 30% load.

## The mechanics that trip people up

Whichever side you land on, World Cup tickets are mobile only. No PDF, no printout. Your ticket shows up as a live QR code inside the official FIFA World Cup 2026 app, and it appears closer to match day rather than the moment you pay. If you are buying for a group, everyone needs the app and an account.

Two things worth knowing before you commit:

A FIFA transfer is not a resale. If a friend bought the ticket and wants to hand it to you with no money changing hands inside FIFA, that transfer is free, and the recipient has 72 hours to accept before it bounces back to the sender. That is the cheapest legitimate way to move a ticket between two people who trust each other, and it dodges the 30% entirely.

A secondary-market purchase usually still routes through the FIFA app for final delivery. So the convenience gap between official and secondary is smaller than it looks. You are mostly trading a guaranteed reissue against a lower price.

## So which should you actually use?

Use FIFA official resale when the match is hot, inventory is thin, and you want zero risk on a high-stakes purchase. The 30% is the price of certainty, and on a once-in-a-lifetime knockout seat that can be worth it.

Use the secondary market when the match is a neutral group-stage fixture in a big stadium, the kind that is softening right now. With prices down across most of the schedule, the open market plus a smaller buyer fee will frequently come in under FIFA's all-in number. Set a target, watch it, and let the floor come to you.

And if you are buying mid-tournament with a deadline bearing down, do not let urgency pick the platform for you. The whole trap is paying FIFA's 30% on instinct when the same seat sits cheaper one tab over.
