## The worst possible moment to find out your ticket is fake

You fly to a host city. You book the hotel, the parking, the pre-game dinner. You walk up to the gate with your phone out and a kid on your shoulders. And the scanner turns red.

That happened to multiple families at World Cup matches in Philadelphia this week. They'd paid real money for resale tickets through third-party and social-media sellers, and the tickets simply never showed up. No transfer, no PDF, no entry. Just a turnstile and a long, expensive trip home.

FIFA's response was blunt: its official platforms are the only channels that can guarantee a valid ticket and actual delivery. Which is technically true, and also not the whole story — because "buy only from FIFA" isn't realistic advice when FIFA's own face-value prices have gone to the moon and millions of seats are now moving on the resale market anyway.

So let's do the consumer-advocate version instead. The goal isn't to scare you off resale. Resale is fine. Resale is often the better deal. The goal is to make sure the version of resale you use can't strand you at the gate.

## Why this is happening now, specifically

A World Cup is the perfect storm for ticket fraud. You've got first-time buyers who've never used a resale marketplace, sky-high emotional stakes, a tournament-long window for scammers to operate, and a flood of international fans who don't know which platforms are legit in North America.

Add the price pressure. When face value climbs into the thousands, people go hunting for a deal, and "a guy in a Facebook group" or "someone in a WhatsApp fan channel" offering a discount starts to sound reasonable. It isn't. That's the exact channel that left those families outside in Philadelphia.

Here's the part that gets lost in the panic: legitimate resale is often priced reasonably. As of today, one of the cheaper matches on the board — Bosnia & Herzegovina vs. Qatar at Lumen Field — had resale starting around $198 on SeatPick. You don't need to gamble with a stranger to find a fair price. You need to know where to look and what protects you.

## The one rule that prevents almost all of this

Buy resale only from a marketplace that guarantees the ticket.

That's it. That's the whole game. The major platforms — Ticketmaster (including its official FIFA resale), SeatGeek, and StubHub — all carry some form of buyer guarantee: if the ticket doesn't arrive or doesn't scan, you get your money back or a comparable replacement. They can do this because the transaction runs through them, not around them.

A stranger on social media offers none of that. There's no escrow, no guarantee, no recourse. Once you've sent the money by Venmo, Zelle, or PayPal "friends and family," it's gone, and so is your leverage. The discount they're dangling is the bait.

So before you pay anyone, ask one question: if this ticket fails to show, who refunds me? If the answer is "nobody" or "trust me," walk.

## A 30-second gate-safety checklist

Run this before every World Cup purchase. It takes less time than reading the seller's sob story about why they can't make the match.

### 1. Is the seller a real marketplace?

If it's Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, or StubHub, you have a guarantee behind you. If it's a person in a comment section, a DM, or a "fan group," you don't. There's no in-between.

### 2. How does the ticket actually transfer?

Modern tickets are mobile and transfer through the official app or a verified link. If someone wants to send you a screenshot, a printout, or "the account login," that's a red flag — screenshots can be sold ten times over, and account logins can be clawed back after you've paid.

### 3. Did you pay through the platform?

Paying inside the marketplace is what activates the guarantee. The second a "seller" tries to move you off-platform to save on fees, the protection evaporates. That's the entire point of moving you off-platform.

### 4. Did you compare before you committed?

This is the step people skip when they're panicking. The cheapest legitimate source flips constantly between the major platforms, sometimes within the same day. If you buy the first price you see, you usually overpay. If you compare, you don't.
