## SeatGeek vs StubHub: The 2026 Breakdown

Every ticket buyer eventually ends up in the same conversation: SeatGeek or StubHub? Both are massive secondary marketplaces. Both have buyer-guarantee programs. Both charge service fees that can push your final price well above the listed number. And yet the answer to "which one wins" is not the same for every purchase.

This is the honest side-by-side: fees, buyer protection, inventory, when each platform is actually better, and when the right move is to check both before clicking anything.

---

## The Fee Math: What You Actually Pay

This is the first number that matters, and both platforms bury the real total until checkout.

**SeatGeek** charges a buyer service fee that typically runs 10-20% of the ticket price. SeatGeek is one of the few platforms that offers an "all-in" pricing toggle, which shows the fee-inclusive total in search results before you click through. That one feature has meaningfully changed how a lot of buyers interact with the platform — when you see "$87 all-in" instead of "$72 + fees," you make a more informed decision.

**StubHub** charges a buyer service fee that typically runs 15-25% of the ticket price. StubHub's pricing historically defaulted to showing the base price with fees revealed at checkout, though the platform has moved toward all-in display in some markets following regulatory and consumer-pressure campaigns. The spread on their fees is wider, meaning the premium vs the base price can vary significantly by event and seller.

Neither platform's fee is fixed or fully predictable, which is why comparing the total checkout price — not the listed price — is the only reliable method.

| Platform | Typical Buyer Fee | All-in Pricing | Mobile Tickets |
|----------|-------------------|----------------|----------------|
| SeatGeek | 10-20% | Toggle available | Yes |
| StubHub | 15-25% | Some markets | Yes |

**Verdict on fees:** SeatGeek's lower typical ceiling and cleaner all-in display give it a structural edge here. But the ranges overlap. A 10% SeatGeek fee on a $200 ticket is $20. A 15% StubHub fee on the same ticket is $30. That difference matters. Check both before buying anything over $100.

## Buyer Protection: What Happens If Something Goes Wrong

This is the question nobody asks until they need it.

**SeatGeek Buyer Guarantee** covers you if tickets are invalid, don't arrive, or don't match the listing. If a ticket fails at the gate, SeatGeek commits to providing comparable replacement tickets or a full refund. The program applies to all purchases on the platform and is backed by their seller verification process.

**StubHub FanProtect Guarantee** covers the same categories: invalid tickets, non-delivery, and material misrepresentation of seat location. StubHub has been operating their buyer guarantee longer and at higher volume — they've processed more claims and have a larger customer service operation to handle disputes.

In practice: both guarantees are real. Both have been exercised by real buyers who had real problems. The more important question is how quickly and smoothly they resolve disputes — and community reporting on both platforms suggests that StubHub's FanProtect is marginally easier to navigate for high-value tickets (think playoff games, World Cup matches, major tours), partly because their support volume means more trained agents.

**Verdict on buyer protection:** Roughly equivalent, with a slight StubHub edge on dispute resolution for high-stakes purchases. For any ticket over $500, the guarantee fine print is worth reading before you commit.

## Inventory: Who Has More Tickets?

The best buyer protection is useless if the ticket you want isn't listed.

**StubHub** is the largest ticket resale marketplace in the US by volume. For most major events — NFL playoffs, NBA Finals, sold-out concerts, World Cup matches — StubHub will have deeper inventory than any competitor. If the event is big, StubHub almost certainly has it.

**SeatGeek** has grown its inventory significantly through venue and team partnerships, which give it exclusive or first-access inventory on certain events. SeatGeek also aggregates listings from partner exchanges, which can surface options that don't appear on StubHub. For events in cities where SeatGeek has venue deals, the inventory advantage flips.

This is actually one of the better arguments for comparing both every time. SeatGeek may have tickets for your specific section that StubHub doesn't carry (or vice versa), not because one has "more" inventory globally, but because seller preferences and partnership arrangements push different tickets to different platforms.

**Verdict on inventory:** StubHub has more total volume. SeatGeek has strategic gaps filled by partnerships. Neither platform has a monopoly on the best seat for any given event.

## User Experience: Which Is Easier to Use

**SeatGeek** has one of the best ticket-buying interfaces in the industry. The interactive seat map, the Deal Score system (which rates tickets as Great, Good, or Fair based on historical price data for that section), and the all-in pricing toggle all make it easier to make an informed decision without clicking through a half-dozen pages. SeatGeek's mobile app is widely considered the cleaner experience.

**StubHub** has a larger and more established interface that has been iterating for longer. It shows more inventory, which means more visual noise — more listings to parse, more price variation to sort through. The newer StubHub interface is cleaner than it was three years ago, but SeatGeek still leads on first-impression usability.

**Verdict on UX:** SeatGeek wins. The Deal Score alone is a meaningful consumer-advocacy feature — it tells you when you're looking at a fair price, not just any price.

## SeatGeek Wins When...

- You're buying concert or theater tickets in a city where SeatGeek has venue partnerships
- You want clear all-in pricing before committing to a section
- The Deal Score shows "Great Deal" — that's a meaningful signal, not just marketing language
- You're buying tickets in the $75-$300 range where the 5-10% fee difference is real money
- You're comparing and want the most transparent price display

## StubHub Wins When...

- You're buying tickets to a major sporting event where volume matters: NFL playoffs, NBA Finals, World Cup knockout rounds
- The event is high-stakes and you want the most experienced dispute-resolution process behind your guarantee
- SeatGeek's inventory for your specific section is thin or missing
- You've found a seller-listed price that beats anything on SeatGeek after fees

## The Part Nobody Likes to Say: Check Both Every Time

Neither platform is consistently cheaper. We've looked at the same event on both platforms on the same day and seen SeatGeek win by $40 and StubHub win by $30 in adjacent categories. Fee ranges overlap. Seller inventory fluctuates. The platform you used last time is not necessarily the platform with the best price today.

The actual efficient behavior is: search both simultaneously, compare the all-in total (not the listed base price), and buy from whichever one is cheaper for that specific seat in that specific section right now.

Face value plus Ticketmaster's own service fee is often cheaper than resale, particularly for events that haven't yet sold out their official allocation.

## The Bottom Line

**SeatGeek:** Better user experience, cleaner fee display, Deal Score adds real value, typically lower fee ceiling. The default starting point for most concert and theater purchases.

**StubHub:** Deeper inventory, more established buyer guarantee, stronger for high-value sporting events. Worth checking for any purchase over $300.

**Both:** Neither platform is reliably cheaper. The smart move is checking both — or all three — before buying anything.
