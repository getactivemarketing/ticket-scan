## Your Reseller Just Canceled Your Tickets. Here's Your 6-Step Action Plan.

On July 3, the Texas Attorney General launched a formal investigation into StubHub following what his office described as "widespread reports" of World Cup ticket orders being accepted, charged, and then canceled — or never delivered at all. New York and New Jersey AGs had already subpoenaed FIFA directly over pricing and seat-location misrepresentation. The Final is July 19.

If you're one of the buyers who paid hundreds or thousands of dollars and got a cancellation notice instead of a ticket, you're not alone — and you're not powerless. Here's exactly what to do, in order.

---

## Why Resellers Cancel Paid Orders

First, a quick explanation of the mechanics, because understanding this helps you fight it.

Many resale platforms allow sellers to list tickets they don't physically hold yet. A seller might list based on a forward contract, a pending transfer, or pure speculation that they'll be able to source inventory. When demand spikes beyond what they anticipated — or supply dries up at their expected buy price — they cancel, eat the platform penalty, and move on. The buyer is left holding a cancellation notice the week of the event.

This is legal under most platform terms of service. It's also a lousy consumer outcome, which is why three state AGs are now involved.

---

## Step 1: Screenshot Everything Right Now

Before you do anything else, document your entire paper trail.

- **Purchase confirmation email** — the original order, full ticket price, all fees, seat details
- **Cancellation notice** — date, time, stated reason (or lack of one)
- **Your payment method** — which card was charged, the exact amount, the date
- **Any communication from the seller or platform** — every message, every response

Save these to your phone and email them to yourself. If this ends up in a credit card dispute or a state AG complaint, documentation is everything.

---

## Step 2: Know Exactly What the Platform Owes You

Every major resale platform has a buyer guarantee. Here's what each promises when a seller cancels:

**StubHub (FanProtect Guarantee):** StubHub is obligated to deliver comparable or better replacement tickets — same event, same or better value — or issue a full refund including fees. "Comparable" is their call, which is often where disputes start. If replacement seats aren't available, you're owed a full refund.

**SeatGeek (Buyer Guarantee):** Similar structure. SeatGeek will provide comparable replacement tickets or a full refund if the seller cancels. Response times vary; escalate quickly if the event is less than two weeks out.

**Ticketmaster Verified Resale:** Ticketmaster's guarantee applies to verified listings. Cancellations trigger either a replacement or full refund. Their system is tightly integrated with venue inventory, which sometimes makes same-day replacements more realistic.

**Vivid Seats (100% Buyer Guarantee):** Full refund or comparable replacement seats if your tickets aren't delivered as listed.

**The important thing:** these are guarantees, not policies at their discretion. If a seller cancels and the platform won't make you whole, that's breach of their stated guarantee — and it's the grounds for the chargeback and complaint steps below.

---

## Step 3: Contact the Platform Immediately — and Escalate Fast

Don't wait. The window between "cancelation notice" and "event date" is your leverage. Platforms move faster when there's still time to actually help you.

**Day 1:** Use the platform's in-app support or live chat first. Get the cancellation acknowledged in writing. Ask specifically: "What replacement options are available, and what is the timeline for my refund if I decline?"

**Day 2 (if no resolution):** Call. Chat agents often have limited authority; phone agents can usually escalate to a dedicated fulfillment team. Document the call: date, time, name of representative, what was promised.

**Day 3 (if still no resolution):** File a formal dispute via the platform's official dispute process. Most have a form specifically for non-delivery. Submit your documentation from Step 1.

One important caveat on replacement tickets: with 12 days until the Final, resale inventory at comparable sections is genuinely limited. If the platform offers you replacement seats that are worse than what you bought, you are not required to accept them — you can decline and take the refund.

---

## Step 4: File a Credit Card Chargeback (If Needed)

If the platform fails to refund you within a reasonable window — typically 5–7 business days for an event this imminent — contact your credit card issuer.

The grounds are simple: **you paid for goods or services that were not delivered.** This is a standard chargeback category. Most issuers will initiate this within 48 hours of your report.

A few things to know:
- You have up to 60 days from the statement date of the charge to dispute it
- The documentation from Step 1 is exactly what your issuer will ask for
- Debit cards have weaker protections — if you paid with a debit card, prioritize the platform resolution path and file an AG complaint in parallel

## Step 5: File a Complaint — It Actually Matters

Three AGs are already investigating. Your complaint adds to the record.

**Texas AG (especially relevant if your match was at AT&T Stadium or NRG Stadium):** [texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection)

**New York or New Jersey AG (Final is at MetLife in East Rutherford, NJ):** Search "NJAG consumer complaint" or "NY AG consumer complaint" — both have online forms.

**Your own state AG:** Every state has a consumer protection division. A quick search for "[your state] attorney general consumer complaint" will get you there.

**FTC:** [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov) — takes 3 minutes and goes into the federal database.

These complaints matter even if they don't directly get your money back. Patterns of complaints trigger investigations. Investigations trigger fines and policy changes. The buyers who file complaints are the reason this story exists at all.

## Step 6: If You Need Replacement Tickets

You got your refund. Now you need seats. Here's how to approach the replacement buy without getting burned twice.

**Compare platforms before you commit.** The same seat in the same section can vary by 20–30% across StubHub, SeatGeek, and Ticketmaster right now, especially as sellers reprice in response to the AG news. Always see the all-in price — not the listing price — before you buy. Fees add 20–32% on top depending on the platform and the price tier.

**Check the FIFA Official Resale Marketplace.** FIFA's own exchange at FIFA.com/tickets stays open until one hour before kickoff. The tickets are fraud-proof (digitally reissued to your FIFA account). The downside: FIFA charges 15% to buyers and 15% to sellers, so "official" doesn't mean "cheapest." Run the numbers both ways.

**Set a price alert.** If you have a specific match and price in mind, use [TicketScan's price tracking](/dashboard) to monitor all three major platforms simultaneously and get notified when any of them drop to your target. Given the volatility right now, alerts catch windows that don't last.

**Don't panic-buy at the first listing you see.** Prices have moved significantly — in both directions — throughout this tournament. The inventory that "disappeared" when platforms got spooked by the AG news tends to reappear within 48 hours as sellers reprice.

## How to Avoid This Next Time

The resale market has structural problems that aren't going away after July 19. Here's what reduces your exposure on any future purchase:

1. **Prefer platforms with clear non-delivery policies.** All four majors have guarantees, but check what "comparable" means before you buy a specific section.
2. **Buy closer to the event.** Speculative listings are most dangerous months out. The closer to game day, the more likely you're buying from someone who actually holds the ticket.
3. **Compare before you commit.** Not just for price — platform reliability signals are visible in fee structures, transfer speed, and buyer protection specificity.
4. **Pay with a credit card.** Always. The chargeback right is your last-resort safety net.
