## The VIP label is doing a lot of heavy lifting

Concert tickets used to be a fairly simple transaction: choose a seat, pay for it, try not to look at your credit-card statement afterward. Now the same event may offer standard admission, premium seating, platinum pricing, a fan club package, a meet-and-greet, or an “experience” that includes a laminate pass and a commemorative tote bag.

That is not automatically bad. A genuinely great seat or a once-in-a-lifetime backstage moment can be worth real money. The problem is that VIP packaging makes it difficult to answer the only question that matters: **what am I actually getting for the extra cost?**

Foreigner’s newly announced Golden Anniversary VIP package is a useful example. The package is reported at roughly $1,000 and includes sitting onstage for three songs, a pre-show event, a photo, early entry, signed items, and other extras. That might be a dream experience for a lifelong fan. It might also be a very expensive way to buy a keychain you will forget exists by October.

Here is the consumer-friendly test.

## 1. What is the comparable standard ticket?

Start with the ordinary ticket, not the VIP headline price. Search for the same event across Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub, then identify the best reasonable standard seat—not the absolute worst seat in the building and not a fantasy listing with impossible fees.

If a standard ticket in a good section costs $180 and the VIP package costs $1,000, the premium is $820. That is the real decision. You are not deciding whether the package costs four digits; you are deciding whether the experience is worth $820 more than attending the show normally.

This comparison also protects you from a common pricing trick: a VIP package can look competitive beside premium resale seats while still being a poor value against the wider inventory. Compare like with like before the marketing language starts making decisions for you.

## 2. Which perks would you pay for separately?

Make two columns: “I would use this” and “nice, I guess.” Be ruthless.

You may genuinely value a soundcheck, early venue access, a meet-and-greet, or a guaranteed front section. You may not value a lanyard, a signed postcard, a tote bag, or early access to future ticket sales. Those items have a production cost, but they do not necessarily have $50—or $500—of value to you.

The best VIP packages usually have one clear anchor benefit: an unusually good seat, meaningful artist access, or a limited experience that cannot be recreated. The weaker ones stack small souvenirs around an ordinary seat and ask you to confuse quantity with value.

## 3. Is the seat itself actually better?

“VIP” does not always mean “closest.” Some packages prioritize access, merchandise, or a pre-show event while placing buyers in a perfectly average section. Check the seating details, not just the badge.

For a stadium or arena show, compare section, row, sightline, and any obstructed-view note. A $300 standard ticket in a great lower-bowl section may beat a $900 package with a worse view. Conversely, a VIP package can be a strong deal if it guarantees a seat that would cost nearly as much on the resale market by itself.

This is where cross-platform checking matters. A seat that appears “premium” on one marketplace may be listed for less elsewhere, and inventory can move quickly as the event approaches. The badge is not a substitute for a seat map.

## 4. Are you comparing all-in prices?

A VIP price is often presented as a neat package total, while standard and resale tickets may display a lower starting price before fees. That makes the comparison unfair from the first click.

Use the final checkout total whenever possible. Include service fees, delivery charges, taxes, parking, and the travel cost created by the package’s timing. If the VIP event requires arriving hours early, that may mean extra food, transit, or a hotel night.

The reverse is also true: some VIP benefits can replace costs you would otherwise pay. Early entry may help a fan avoid a separate premium upgrade. A bundled photo or exclusive event may be valuable if you were already planning to buy that experience. Put numbers on both sides instead of letting “exclusive” do the accounting.

## 5. What happens if your plans change?

Read the transfer, refund, and package rules before buying. VIP perks are frequently non-transferable even when the ticket itself can be transferred. Some packages require the original purchaser to be present, and some benefits disappear if you arrive late.

That matters because an expensive ticket is also an expensive commitment. A standard resale ticket may be easier to transfer if work, illness, or travel problems get in the way. A VIP package may be the right choice for a fan who is certain they can attend—but a terrible choice for a buyer who is still “probably” free that night.

## A simple VIP value formula

Try this quick calculation:

**VIP premium = VIP all-in price − best comparable standard ticket all-in price**

Then subtract only the value of perks you would actually buy or use. If the remaining premium feels uncomfortable, it is too high. No amount of gold foil on the laminate changes that.

You can also set a ceiling before looking at listings. For example: “I will pay up to $400 for a great seat, or $650 if the package includes a real artist experience.” A target price keeps scarcity language from turning a want into an emergency.

## The bottom line

VIP concert tickets are worth it when the centerpiece benefit is genuinely rare, the seat is clearly competitive, and the all-in premium fits your budget. They are not worth it when most of the price buys filler merchandise, vague access, or a label that sounds better than the view.
