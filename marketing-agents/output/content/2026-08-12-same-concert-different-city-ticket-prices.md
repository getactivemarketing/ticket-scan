## The artist is the same. The ticket math is not.

A recent ticket-price story about Rod Stewart makes a point that concert shoppers learn the expensive way: the price of seeing an artist is not one number.

An [Axios report](https://www.axios.com/local/des-moines/2026/08/07/rod-stewart-iowa-state-fair-ticket-prices) described a fan paying $931 for a fifth-row Rod Stewart ticket on Long Island. The report compared that purchase with Stewart's planned August 14 appearance with Richard Marx at the Iowa State Fair, where tickets were available at prices far below that purchase. The Iowa show was canceled on August 11 because of medical issues, so it is no longer a live buying opportunity—but the comparison still shows the underlying point: same headliner, same tour cycle, very different market.

That does not mean every Iowa ticket was a bargain, or that every expensive seat was a rip-off. It does mean a single search in a single city can create a dangerously incomplete picture of what a tour costs.

If a show looks impossible at your home venue, do not immediately close the tab—or empty your savings account. First, check whether the market is telling you the concert is expensive everywhere, or merely expensive there.

## Why prices change from city to city

Concert pricing is a messy blend of demand, supply, venue economics, timing, and seat quality. Here are the biggest variables.

### Local demand

An artist may have a stronger fan base in one city, or that city may be the only stop within a large regional radius. When thousands of fans are competing for one nearby date, sellers have more room to push prices upward.

The reverse is also true. A date in a smaller market, a city with several competing events, or a location that requires a longer drive can have softer demand. That can create better inventory without changing the performer.

### Date and calendar pressure

Friday and Saturday shows usually attract a premium because fans do not have to take time off work or school. Holiday weekends, festival dates, and the first or last night of a tour can also carry extra hype.

A Tuesday show may be less glamorous but much easier on the wallet. So can a late-summer fair date, where the ticket is part of a broader event rather than a standalone arena production.

### Venue capacity and layout

A 20,000-seat arena and a 6,000-seat theater do not create the same supply. Neither does an outdoor fairground and a premium indoor venue.

Capacity is only the first layer. Sightlines, floor configuration, stage layout, club access, and obstructed-view inventory all change what “a good seat” means. Comparing a fifth-row arena ticket with the cheapest seat at a fair is not apples to apples. Comparing the best acceptable seat at both can still reveal where the value is.

### Primary pricing versus resale pricing

The first ticketing site you visit may show primary inventory, official resale, or a mix of both. Those listings can follow different pricing rules. Resellers may lower a price because they cannot attend, while primary inventory may remain expensive because the tour or venue has not adjusted its price.

That is why “Ticketmaster is expensive” or “resale is always cheaper” is not a useful buying strategy. The cheapest source changes by event, date, section, and moment.

## The five-minute nearby-city check

Before you decide that a concert is out of budget, run this comparison.

### 1. List every realistic date

Include the closest weekend date, any weekday date, and stops within a reasonable driving or flight radius. Be honest about travel costs. A $40 ticket that requires a $300 hotel is not a $40 concert.

### 2. Set a seat standard

Choose the minimum experience you will accept: upper level with a clear view, lower bowl, floor, aisle seat, or a specific section. A price comparison is meaningless if one listing is a premium seat and the other is behind the stage.

### 3. Check all three major marketplaces

Search Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub for the same date and comparable seat tier. TicketScan puts those sources next to each other so you can identify whether the difference is coming from the city, the seller, or the platform.

### 4. Write down the all-in total

The number that matters is the number charged for the ticket, fees, delivery, and taxes—not the tempting number displayed before checkout. Record the total for the same ticket quantity. Two seats together can have a very different per-seat price than one ticket.

### 5. Add travel to the decision

Use a simple total-cost calculation:

all-in tickets + gas or airfare + parking + hotel + food = actual concert cost

The $931 local ticket may still beat a $180 ticket in another state if the trip costs $1,000. But do the math before the countdown timer convinces you that the first listing is your only option.

## When a cheaper city is actually worth it

A nearby-city ticket is worth considering when the price gap is large, you can make the trip without missing work, and the venue offers comparable sightlines. It is especially attractive for a weekend date, a concert you already planned to travel for, or a show with flexible resale inventory.

It is less attractive when the ticket is cheap because the view is obstructed, the transfer arrives late, or the event has strict entry requirements. Buyer protection and delivery timing belong in the comparison, too. Saving $200 is not a win if the ticket never reaches your phone.

## The consumer takeaway from the Rod Stewart example

The useful lesson is not “never pay $931.” Some fans value a fifth-row seat enough to pay for it. The lesson is to know what you are buying and what alternatives exist before you commit.

Concert pricing rewards shoppers who widen the search just enough: one or two additional dates, a realistic seat tier, and multiple marketplaces. You do not need to become a full-time ticket trader. You need a baseline, an all-in budget, and enough patience to notice when the market is asking you to overreact.
