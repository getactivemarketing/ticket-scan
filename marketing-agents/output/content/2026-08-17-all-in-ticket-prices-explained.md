## The number on the first screen is not the price

Ticket shopping has developed its own little magic trick: show you a number that looks manageable, then add enough fees at checkout to make you wonder whether the seat comes with a small apartment.

The fix is simple, even if the marketplaces make it feel unnecessarily ceremonial. Compare the all-in price: the amount you actually pay for the same number of tickets, in a genuinely comparable location, delivered under terms you can accept.

That distinction matters for everything from a regular-season baseball game to the 2026 US Open. The US Open runs August 23 through September 13, with Fan Week and qualifying sessions before the main draw. Official ticket pages list multiple ticket types, sessions, and venues within the tennis center, so “a US Open ticket” is not one product or one price. The same is true of a concert floor seat versus an upper-level seat, or a playoff game in Section 112 versus Section 212.

## What “all-in” should include

For a useful comparison, capture the final total for your whole order—not just the advertised price per ticket. Your all-in number should account for:

- The ticket price for each seat
- Service or marketplace fees
- Delivery or mobile-transfer charges, if any
- Taxes or other mandatory charges shown before payment
- The number of tickets you are buying

Some marketplaces display fees earlier than others, and policies can vary by event. That means a headline price is a starting point, not a verdict. If one site shows $140 and another shows $152, you still do not know which is cheaper until both listings are evaluated on the same fee basis.

Also record whether the displayed amount is per ticket or for the entire order. A surprisingly common source of confusion is multiplying a number that already includes two tickets—or forgetting to multiply one that does not.

## Compare the same ticket, not merely the same event

The fairest comparison unit is not “Ticketmaster versus SeatGeek.” It is “this event, this date, this quantity, this seat range, and this delivery requirement across Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub.”

Before you compare, define your minimum acceptable ticket:

1. Choose the event and exact date.
2. Set the number of tickets you need.
3. Pick a section or a seat-quality range.
4. Decide whether an obstructed view, standing room, or restricted view is acceptable.
5. Set a maximum all-in budget.
6. Note whether you need instant delivery or can wait for a mobile transfer.

If you skip this step, the cheapest listing may simply be a worse product. A $95 seat behind the stage is not automatically a better deal than a $112 seat with a clear view. Nor is a $300 ticket for one session comparable to a $300 package covering several sessions.

## Why the same section can cost different amounts

Price differences across marketplaces are normal. They do not automatically mean one company is hiding a mistake or that one seller is always cheapest.

Supply is fragmented. One marketplace may have a primary ticket, another may have a fan resale listing, and a third may show a different seller’s inventory. Sellers also set their own asking prices, so two seats in the same section can have different rows, views, delivery times, or restrictions.

Demand changes too. A Saturday concert, a rivalry game, a finals session, or a newly announced artist can move faster than a Tuesday event. A seller who needs to unload a ticket may undercut the market, while another seller may price for the last available seat.

Fees add another layer. A listing that leads on the first screen can lose after service charges are applied. Conversely, a slightly higher listed price can become the better buy if it has clearer delivery terms, a better row, or fewer surprises at checkout.

This is why there is no permanently cheapest ticket platform. The winner can change by event, seat, quantity, and timestamp.

## A five-minute all-in comparison

Use this process whenever you are close to buying.

### 1. Search the exact event

Confirm the venue, date, matchup or artist, and session. For a multi-day event such as the US Open, distinguish Fan Week, qualifying, day sessions, evening sessions, and finals weekend.

### 2. Match quantity and seat quality

Compare two tickets with two tickets. Keep the section or acceptable range consistent. If the sites use different labels, inspect the map and row rather than trusting a color-coded “good deal” badge.

### 3. Write down the displayed total

Record whether the number is before or after fees, whether it is per ticket, and whether taxes or delivery charges remain. A quick note on your phone is enough; the goal is to stop your brain from comparing three different numbers at once.

### 4. Check the purchase terms

Verify transfer timing, refund or cancellation rules, restrictions, and the marketplace’s buyer protection. A lower total is not useful if the ticket arrives after you need to enter the venue.

### 5. Check again before paying

Inventory and prices can change. A price alert can help you watch an event without refreshing tabs like a stressed-out day trader. When the target price arrives, compare the current all-in totals again and confirm the seat still meets your requirements.

## When the cheapest all-in price is not the right choice

Price is important, but it is not the only value metric. Paying a little more can be reasonable for a materially better view, instant delivery, adjacent seats, or clearer buyer protection. The key is making that trade deliberately instead of discovering it after checkout.

For a group, multiply small differences. Saving $18 per ticket is $72 for four people. That is not pocket change; it is parking, dinner, or the part of the trip that does not involve arguing with a service-fee screen.
