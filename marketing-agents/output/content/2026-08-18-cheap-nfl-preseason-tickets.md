## The preseason is where NFL ticket math gets weird

The NFL is back on the calendar, but preseason tickets do not follow the same pricing logic as regular-season games. You are still walking into the same stadium, staring at the same giant video board, and paying for the same parking situation. The difference is that the starters may spend more time in baseball caps than shoulder pads.

That gap creates an opportunity. Preseason games can be one of the cheapest ways to experience an NFL stadium, especially when you care more about the live atmosphere than whether every star plays four quarters. But the opportunity disappears quickly when sellers price an exhibition like a divisional showdown.

Current coverage of the Los Angeles market illustrates the spread: some preseason get-in prices have been reported below $25, while stronger matchups and better sections can cost far more. The lesson is not that every preseason ticket is cheap. It is that the price depends heavily on the game, the section, the timing, and the marketplace you check.

## 1. Start with the matchup, not the logo

“NFL tickets” is too broad a search to make a good buying decision. The visiting team matters. So does the home team’s local demand, the players expected to appear, and whether the game is a weekend event for families or a midweek stop on a crowded sports calendar.

Make a quick matchup score before you shop:

- **Lower-demand game:** early preseason, weekday, fewer recognizable starters expected
- **Middle-demand game:** weekend kickoff, young players competing for roster spots, normal local interest
- **Higher-demand game:** final preseason game, regional rivalry, star-heavy lineup, or a special stadium event

The higher the score, the less likely you are to find a dramatic last-minute discount. If your goal is simply to see an NFL game in person, target the lower-demand matchup. If you want a specific quarterback or rookie, accept that you are buying player availability risk along with the ticket.

## 2. Compare all-in prices across marketplaces

The number in a search result is not the number that hits your credit card. A $35 ticket can become a $55 ticket after service fees, delivery charges, and taxes. Another marketplace may show a higher list price but a lower total at checkout.

Check Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub side by side. Record four details for the same approximate seat location:

1. Listing price
2. Fees and estimated taxes
3. Final all-in total
4. Section, row, and ticket delivery method

This matters because “cheapest” is meaningless if one result is an end-zone seat and the other is midfield, or if one price excludes fees. ## 3. Buy the right section for the experience you want

Preseason is usually a poor time to stretch for premium seats. The game itself is less predictable, so the value of a perfect view can be lower than it is for a playoff chase or a rivalry game.

For a first visit, upper-level sideline sections often offer the best balance of view and price. End-zone seats can be cheaper, but check the angle before buying: a low end-zone location may make it harder to follow plays at the opposite goal line. If you are bringing children, prioritize sightlines, shade, and easy concourse access over being as close as possible.

One practical test: compare the price difference between an upper-level sideline seat and a lower-level corner seat after fees. If the upgrade is modest, it may be worth it. If the lower-level seat costs double for an exhibition game, keep the money for food, parking, or—radical idea—another game.

## 4. Use timing, but do not rely on a single “best day”

Preseason inventory changes as schedules, injuries, and roster news change. Prices can soften when casual buyers lose interest, but they can also jump if a popular player is announced for an extended appearance.

A sensible buying window is often one to three weeks before kickoff, then a second check 24 to 48 hours before the game. That gives you time to see whether prices are drifting down without forcing you into a last-minute panic purchase.

Set a target price before you start browsing. For example, you might decide that an upper-level sideline seat is worth $45 all-in and a lower-level seat is worth $85 all-in. If the market misses your number, do not let a countdown timer make the decision for you. Exhibition football will survive your absence.

The exception is a game with limited inventory or a specific player you must see. In that case, buy when the price is acceptable rather than gambling on a drop that may never come.

## 5. Watch for the hidden costs around the ticket

The ticket is only one part of the trip. Before calling a $20 listing a bargain, add:

- Parking or public transit
- Food and drinks
- Ticket transfer or delivery restrictions
- Potential hotel costs for an out-of-town game
- The value of your time if the seat is difficult to reach

An inexpensive ticket can still become an expensive night. On the other hand, a slightly higher ticket price at a nearby stadium may beat a cheaper listing that requires a hotel and surge-priced parking.

## The preseason buying checklist

Before checkout, confirm that you have compared the same event and date, checked the all-in total, reviewed the seat map, and looked at the expected player availability. Then ask the only question that matters: “Would I pay this amount if the starters leave after the first quarter?”

If the answer is yes, buy with confidence. If the answer is no, set a price alert and wait. The goal is not to win a screenshot of the lowest listing. It is to have a good night at a price that does not make you resent the nachos.
