## The best US Open ticket depends on what you actually want to see

The 2026 US Open gets underway in New York this week, which means the annual ticket question has arrived: do you want to watch a particular star, spend an entire day wandering the grounds, or simply get inside the tournament without donating a small vacation budget to the ticketing industry?

Those are three different purchases. Treating them as interchangeable is how fans end up paying stadium-session prices for a seat they barely use—or buying a cheap grounds pass while expecting a guaranteed view of the biggest match of the day.

The qualifying rounds and Fan Week run August 24–27. The main draw runs August 30 through September 13 at the USTA Billie Jean King National Tennis Center in Flushing Meadows, Queens. The official US Open ticket page lists day and evening sessions across Arthur Ashe Stadium, Louis Armstrong Stadium, Grandstand, and the grounds. Ticketmaster is the tournament’s official ticketing partner, but “official” does not automatically mean “cheapest.”

Here is the value-focused way to shop.

## Start with Fan Week if your priority is tennis, not a specific seat

Fan Week is the obvious budget play. Entry is free, but this year fans need to register for a Fan Access Pass. The week includes qualifying matches, player activity, and special events before the main draw begins. Some premium events require separate tickets.

This is a particularly good fit if you are bringing a family, want to sample the tournament atmosphere, or care more about seeing live competition than checking a famous player off your list. Qualifying also has a built-in upside: the matches matter enormously to the players, even if the names are less familiar to casual fans.

The catch is predictability. A free pass does not guarantee Arthur Ashe access or a specific player. If your entire trip depends on seeing one athlete, Fan Week is a fun bonus—not a complete ticket strategy.

## Grounds passes offer the best “most tennis per dollar” tradeoff

During the main draw, a grounds pass generally gives you access to the outer courts and the wider tournament campus, with unreserved access in designated areas. It is the right choice for fans who enjoy hopping between matches, discovering players before they become household names, and treating the US Open like a full-day festival with rackets.

Grounds access is also less exposed to one bad draw. A stadium ticket can lose some of its appeal if the player you came to see is eliminated, withdrawn, or moved to another court. On the grounds, you have options.

But do not confuse flexibility with a guaranteed headline match. Grounds passes do not give you a reserved seat in Arthur Ashe, and capacity rules or court popularity can limit where you can stand or sit. Check the exact ticket description before purchasing, especially for evening sessions and later rounds.

## Stadium sessions are worth the premium only when the match matters to you

Arthur Ashe and Louis Armstrong tickets buy certainty: a reserved seat, a scheduled session, and a better chance of seeing the tournament’s biggest names. That certainty is valuable for a once-in-a-lifetime trip or a fan who has one non-negotiable match in mind.

It is also where prices can get silly quickly. Finals, semifinals, opening-night spectacles, and sessions featuring a major draw can carry a steep demand premium. Two seats in the same stadium can have dramatically different prices based on session, section, row, and resale timing.

Before paying that premium, ask one unfashionable but useful question: am I buying the match, or am I buying the building? If the answer is the building, compare a grounds pass or an earlier-round stadium session. If the answer is a specific player, verify the schedule and resale rules before committing. Tennis schedules change; your credit-card statement remains annoyingly permanent.

## Day versus evening: compare the experience, not just the number

Day sessions are often better for fans who want multiple matches, outer-court access, and more time on site. Evening sessions can deliver the atmosphere people associate with the US Open, especially when a marquee match lands in Arthur Ashe, but they may offer fewer total hours of tennis.

The cheapest listed ticket is not automatically the lowest-cost outing. Add transportation from Manhattan or elsewhere in New York, food, and the possibility that you will want another session after a short evening. A slightly higher-priced day ticket can be the better value if it replaces several smaller activities. Conversely, a local fan may get more utility from a night session after work.

Think in terms of cost per hour and probability of seeing what you want—not just the headline ticket price.

## Primary and resale prices should be checked together

The official US Open site and Ticketmaster are the natural starting points for primary inventory. They show the event details and ticket types clearly, which matters when you are comparing a reserved stadium seat with general grounds access.

Resale can still be worth checking. A ticket holder who cannot attend may price below the current primary listing, while a high-demand session may be marked up substantially. The cheapest marketplace can change by event, section, and time of day, so a single-source search gives you only one slice of the market.

Use the same checklist on every platform:

1. Compare the all-in total, including fees and delivery.
2. Confirm the session date, start time, stadium, and seat location.
3. Check whether the ticket is a grounds pass, a reserved seat, or a hospitality package.
4. Review the marketplace’s buyer protection and transfer terms.
5. Save the event and set a target price instead of panic-buying the first acceptable listing.

That last step matters most for fans with flexible dates. A price alert lets you wait for a meaningful drop while keeping an eye on inventory across Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub.

## A simple decision guide

Choose Fan Week if you want the lowest-cost entry and enjoy qualifying or special events.

Choose a grounds pass if you want a full day of tennis and are happy to explore rather than follow one guaranteed match.

Choose a stadium session if a particular player, round, or reserved seat is the point of the trip.

Choose resale only after checking the primary listing and the all-in total on competing marketplaces.

The U.S. Open is a massive event, which is good news for shoppers: there are more session types and more ways to build a worthwhile day than the premium Arthur Ashe headline suggests. Start with the experience you want, set a budget, and compare the actual ticket—not the marketing label attached to it.
