## A $100 US Open ticket is real. That does not mean every ticket is worth $100.

New York City and the USTA announced a limited offer of 1,000 tickets to the 2026 US Open for New York City residents. The tickets are priced at $100 and go on sale Wednesday, August 26, at 10 a.m. through the official US Open ticket page. The offer covers Main Draw sessions, including access connected to Arthur Ashe Stadium, Louis Armstrong Stadium, and the grounds.

That is a genuinely useful break from the usual major-event pricing circus. It is also a first-come, first-served offer with limited inventory, so the practical question is not simply, “Are there $100 tickets?” It is: “What can I actually see for $100, and what will comparable tickets cost elsewhere?”

That distinction matters because a cheap ticket is only a good deal when the seat, session, fees, and timing make sense for you.

## What the $100 offer includes

The city and USTA say the program is intended to make the tournament more accessible to New Yorkers. The available tickets cover Main Draw sessions scheduled during the tournament, and the offer includes seats in Arthur Ashe Stadium and Louis Armstrong Stadium as well as grounds passes.

There are a few details to keep in mind before you sprint for the checkout button:

- The offer is limited to New York City residents.
- Tickets are first-come, first-served.
- Availability depends on the session and remaining inventory.
- The $100 price may not be the final amount after applicable fees or checkout terms.
- A grounds pass and a reserved stadium seat are different experiences, even if both get you through the gates.

The headline price is useful. The session details are where the value lives.

## How to compare US Open sessions like a deal hunter

The US Open is not one event with one price curve. It is a long run of day sessions, night sessions, early-round matches, later-round matches, singles, doubles, and grounds access. Demand changes dramatically depending on who is playing and how far the tournament has progressed.

### 1. Start with the matchups, not the stadium name

Arthur Ashe Stadium has the biggest name recognition, but the best tennis value may be on a session with strong matches in Louis Armstrong or on the grounds. A famous player in a night session can command a premium, while an earlier session may offer more total tennis for less money.

If your goal is to see elite competition rather than a particular player, keep several sessions open. Flexibility is one of the few advantages buyers have over dynamic pricing systems.

### 2. Check whether the ticket is a seat or a pass

A reserved seat answers one question: where will you sit? A grounds pass answers another: how freely can you move around the site and watch matches on smaller courts?

Neither is automatically better. A grounds pass can be fantastic during early rounds when multiple matches are happening at once. A reserved Ashe ticket is more valuable if you care about the largest court, a specific matchup, or a night-session atmosphere.

Compare the experience, not just the number printed beside the ticket.

### 3. Calculate the all-in price

Ticket fees have a talent for appearing after you have already emotionally moved into your seat. Before comparing the official offer with Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, StubHub, or any other marketplace, write down the full checkout total.

Use the same checklist for every listing:

- Ticket price
- Service and delivery fees
- Taxes, if shown
- Session date and start time
- Section, row, and seat type
- Transfer or delivery restrictions

Comparing a $100 face-value ticket with a $135 resale total is not a fair comparison unless you know both tickets provide a similar session and seat experience.

## Why resale prices can still be worth checking

“Official” and “cheapest” are not synonyms. The official $100 offer is an excellent reference point, but limited inventory means it may disappear quickly or may not cover the session you want.

Resale prices can move in either direction. A high-demand night session may be far above the city offer. An early-round session, an inconvenient weekday, or a ticket from a fan who can no longer attend may appear below another seller’s price. Inventory also changes as the tournament gets closer.

That is why a single-source search can leave money on the table. Ticketmaster may show the primary inventory. SeatGeek and StubHub may show resale inventory with different seat locations and fees. A side-by-side comparison gives you a faster read on whether you are paying for a better seat, a better session, or merely a more urgent-looking checkout page.

## A simple buying strategy for August 26

If you are eligible for the $100 offer, check the official US Open page first and have your account details ready before the sale time. Keep a short list of acceptable sessions rather than fixating on one exact seat.

If the offer is sold out, do not panic-buy the first resale listing you see. Search the same session across multiple marketplaces, compare the all-in totals, and look at nearby sessions on the calendar. A one-day shift can change the price substantially.

If you are not ready to buy today, set a target price and track the sessions you would genuinely attend. A price alert turns “I should check again sometime” into a specific notification, which is much less likely to end with a 1 a.m. impulse purchase and a seat behind a pillar.

## The bottom line

The $100 US Open ticket offer is a strong reminder that major sporting events do not have one universal market price. There is the official offer, the primary inventory, the resale market, and the value of the actual session. Your best deal comes from comparing all four.

Start with the official program. Then verify the session, calculate the all-in cost, and compare similar tickets before buying. That takes a few extra minutes and can save far more than a promotional headline promises.
