## The ticket listing you bought may not have existed yet

California just took aim at one of the ticket industry's least charming tricks: selling a ticket before the seller actually has the ticket.

On September 27, 2026, Gov. Gavin Newsom approved Assembly Bill 1349, a consumer-protection law covering ticket sellers and resale marketplaces. The California Legislative Information page lists the measure as Chapter 474. Among other changes, it prohibits “speculative ticket sales”—the practice commonly called ghost ticketing—and requires resale marketplaces to take reasonable measures to prevent them.

That matters because a polished listing can create the impression that a real, deliverable seat is waiting behind the checkout button. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the seller is betting that they can acquire the seat later, at a price that still leaves room for profit. If that bet fails, the fan is the one standing outside the venue with a hotel reservation and a very expensive lesson.

The law is a meaningful step. It is not a force field around your wallet.

## What California's new law targets

The final text covers more than one flavor of ticket nonsense.

### 1. Speculative, or “ghost,” tickets

The law generally describes a speculative sale as advertising, listing, marketing, offering, accepting payment for, or selling a ticket when the seller does not have the ticket or a contractual right to obtain it. A seller cannot simply put a hypothetical seat on the market and hope the supply appears later.

The bill also says a seller that breaks the rules and fails to provide the ticket can be liable for twice the contracted ticket price, plus certain nonrefundable expenses and legal costs. That is a serious consequence. It is also a remedy after something has gone wrong, not a substitute for checking a listing before you buy.

### 2. Bots and limit-dodging

AB 1349 also makes it unlawful to use software or other methods to evade posted ticket limits, electronic queues, presale restrictions, account limits, or similar distribution controls. In plain English: creating a small army of accounts and email addresses to vacuum up inventory is not a clever life hack.

### 3. Fake-looking ticket websites and misleading claims

The law addresses websites, domains, logos, and advertisements that could make a reasonable buyer think they are purchasing directly from a venue, artist, rights holder, or authorized seller when they are actually buying from a reseller.

It also restricts claims that a resale ticket is from an authorized source, that an event is sold out when authorized inventory remains, or that a listing reflects a specific seat when the seller has no reasonable basis for that claim.

Those rules attack a very real problem: consumers are often making a decision before they have had time to work out who is actually selling the ticket.

## What the law does not fix overnight

California's law does not make every ticket on every platform the same price. It does not end dynamic pricing. It does not guarantee that the official seller will have the cheapest seat. It also does not mean every resale listing is a scam; many are ordinary fans or season-ticket holders trying to recover money for an event they cannot attend.

The measure's final language drew criticism from independent venue advocates, while resale-industry representatives said it still bans speculative ticketing on marketplaces. Newsom's signing statement encouraged lawmakers and stakeholders to revisit parts of the policy. In other words, the consumer-protection direction is clear, but the industry argument over how the rules apply is not finished.

For buyers, that is a reason to be more precise—not more cynical. Ask three questions: Does the seller have the ticket? Is the listing clear about whether it is primary or resale inventory? What will the all-in price be before you commit?

## The smartest buying workflow after AB 1349

### Start with the event, not the marketplace

Search for the event by artist, team, date, and venue. A reseller's search page may be optimized to capture your click, not to show you the full market. Confirm that the event is real, the date is correct, and the venue is the one you intend to visit.

### Compare primary and resale inventory side by side

Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub can show different inventory at different prices. One platform may have the cheapest upper-level seat; another may have a better lower-level option after fees. There is no permanent “cheapest site” trophy. Sellers, supply, demand, and timing keep moving the goalposts.

This is where a comparison tool earns its keep: it gives you a baseline before urgency turns a $90 decision into a $190 decision. Treat any “only two left” message as information to verify, not a command from the ticket gods.

### Check the final price, not the seductive first number

The headline price is often not the checkout price. Compare the total for the same quantity of tickets and similar locations. If one listing appears dramatically cheaper, check whether it is a partial view, restricted entry, obstructed seat, or a price that excludes mandatory fees.

### Save the evidence

Keep the confirmation email, listing details, seat information, and payment record. If a listing is materially different from what was promised, those details matter. A screenshot of the marketing language can be more useful than your memory after the confetti falls.

### Use an alert instead of panic-buying

If the event is not tonight, set a target price and watch it. Prices can move when inventory changes, when an event gets closer, or when sellers decide that owning an unsold ticket is worse than taking a smaller margin. A price alert gives you a chance to react to a real drop instead of an artificial countdown clock.

## The bottom line

AB 1349 is good news for fans because it draws a brighter line around tickets that were never really available. But laws work at the system level; your buying decision still happens one event, one seat, and one checkout page at a time.

The practical rule remains gloriously unexciting: verify the seller, compare the inventory, inspect the all-in total, and do not let a blinking timer make your financial decisions for you.
