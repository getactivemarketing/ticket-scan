## The announcement is not the onsale

The first thing to know about a new tour announcement: the headline is not the ticket information.

When an artist adds dates, the announcement may tell you the cities and venues but leave out the details that matter at checkout. There may be an artist presale, a venue presale, a credit-card presale, a general onsale, and a separate resale page. Those are different doors into the same building, and they do not always open at the same time.

That distinction matters this week. A$AP Rocky has announced five arena shows in Australia and New Zealand for December 2026, according to [a report from Effingham Radio](https://www.effinghamradio.com/2026/10/14/aap-rocky-announces-australia-and-new-zealand-tour-dates-for-december-2026/). Fans searching immediately will encounter a mix of announcement coverage, signup pages, venue calendars, and listings that may not yet represent the official primary sale.

Here is the seven-minute check I use before clicking buy.

## 1. Confirm the event, venue, and date

Start with the boring details. Boring is good here.

Check the artist's official site, the venue's calendar, and the primary ticketing page. Make sure all three agree on:

- City and country
- Venue name
- Date and local start time
- Whether the listing is for the full concert or a VIP package
- Whether the date is an announced date or a confirmed onsale

Tour announcements can change. A venue can also list multiple events for the same artist, including an extra date, a fan-club event, or a package that is not the standard concert ticket. If a page is vague about the building or the date, close it and verify the event somewhere else.

## 2. Write down every onsale time

Do not trust a search result to remember the time zone for you. Add the presale and general onsale to your calendar with the city’s local time, then convert it to your own time zone.

Keep the sale stages separate:

### Artist or fan-club presale

This may require a signup, membership, or code. A signup confirmation is not the same thing as a ticket guarantee. Read the email carefully for the access link, start time, and any purchase limit.

### Venue or promoter presale

The venue may distribute its own code or use a separate ticketing page. Check the venue’s official account and website rather than copying a code from a random comment thread.

### General onsale

This is the public sale time. Put the exact link in your notes ahead of time. During a popular onsale, searching for the link while the queue is open is a good way to miss the queue.

### Official resale

If the show sells out, an official resale channel may appear later. Treat it as a separate inventory source, and read the marketplace’s delivery and refund rules before paying.

## 3. Know what kind of listing you are seeing

Not every result that contains an artist’s name is a normal concert ticket. Watch for labels such as:

- VIP experience
- Hospitality package
- Meet and greet
- Travel package
- Parking only
- Resale listing
- Waitlist or registration

Those products can be legitimate, but they are not interchangeable. A VIP package may include a ticket, or it may describe extras that require a separate ticket. A travel package may bundle a hotel and transport. A parking listing gets you nowhere near the stage unless you already have admission.

Read the item description all the way through. If the seat location, delivery method, or ticket type is missing, that is a reason to pause—not a reason to assume the best.

## 4. Compare the final total, not the first number

The number that gets your attention in a listing is often not the number that reaches your card. Before buying, look for service fees, delivery fees, taxes, currency conversion, and any order or handling charge.

For an international tour, currency deserves its own check. A ticket listed in Australian dollars or New Zealand dollars can look cheaper than expected until your bank’s conversion rate and foreign-transaction fee appear. Check the seller’s final checkout total in your home currency if it provides one. If it does not, do the conversion yourself before you commit.

Also check whether the seller shows the all-in total before payment. If the fees stay hidden until the final screen, take a screenshot of the listing details and review the total before submitting the order.

## 5. Be suspicious of urgency that comes from nowhere

Popular shows create real urgency. Scammers create artificial urgency on top of it.

Red flags include a seller demanding payment by gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a direct message; a listing that promises a ticket before the official onsale; a social account with no verifiable connection to the venue; and a page that uses a countdown without identifying the ticket seller.

Use the official artist, venue, promoter, and ticketing links as your trail back to the event. If a stranger sends you a “better link,” verify it independently instead of trusting the message.

## 6. Save the confirmation and the rules

After you buy, save the confirmation email, order number, delivery terms, and refund policy. Screenshot the seat details if the seller allows it. Mobile tickets can be delivered later, but “later” should still have a stated window.

If the event is postponed, moved, or canceled, the policy you agreed to at checkout is the document that matters. Keep it.

## 7. Use the venue as your cheat sheet

The venue page is often more useful than a generic search result. It can tell you the entrance layout, section names, accessibility details, parking options, and the event calendar. Those details help you avoid buying the wrong product and make it easier to judge whether a seat description actually makes sense.

## The short version

Before buying tickets for a newly announced tour:

1. Confirm the venue, city, date, and local time.
2. Separate presale access from the general onsale.
3. Identify whether the listing is a standard ticket, VIP package, or resale listing.
4. Review the all-in total and currency conversion.
5. Pay through a verified ticket seller, not a stranger in your messages.
6. Save the confirmation and refund terms.

The fun part is finding out your favorite artist is coming to town. The less fun part is discovering that you bought parking, a waitlist spot, or a ticket for the wrong date. Do the seven-minute check first.
