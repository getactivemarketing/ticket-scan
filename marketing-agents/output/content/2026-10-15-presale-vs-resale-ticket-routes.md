## A presale signup is not a ticket

The modern ticket rush has a predictable plot: an artist announces a special show, fans race to register, the registration page buckles, and resale listings appear before the official onsale has even finished explaining itself.

That is the backdrop to RAYE’s one-night Royal Albert Hall show on October 23. [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c8vgxpwlx9wko) that the 5,000-seat event drew 1.8 million sign-ups and that RAYE said artificial-intelligence bots had overwhelmed registration. The [Royal Albert Hall’s event page](https://www.royalalberthall.com/tickets/events/2026/raye) is the useful reality check: it identifies the venue, event, and official event context.

The lesson is bigger than one concert. “I registered” can mean you joined a list. “I got selected” can mean you received access to a sale. Neither phrase automatically means you own a ticket.

Here are the four routes fans are most likely to encounter, compared without the ticket-industry fog machine.

## 1. Artist or fan-club presale

An artist presale is an early opportunity tied to an artist’s mailing list, fan club, app, or registration page. Some use codes. Some use a unique link. Some select accounts or send access instructions shortly before the sale.

The upside is timing: you may get a chance to buy before the general public. The catch is that access is usually limited, inventory can be tiny, and registration does not reserve a seat. A huge registration list can also create a false sense that everyone is competing for the full venue. Promoters may hold back sections for later sale, production needs, sponsors, or other presales.

Treat the confirmation email like a set of instructions, not a receipt. Check the sale date, local start time, purchase limit, official URL, and whether the message says “request,” “register,” “invite,” or “purchase.” Those words are not interchangeable.

## 2. Venue or promoter primary sale

The primary sale is the original ticket release handled by the venue, promoter, or its authorized ticketing partner. It is the cleanest place to confirm that the event exists and that you are buying the ticket product described on the official page.

For a show with multiple presales, the primary seller may open inventory in stages. That means an empty screen during one sale is not proof that the event is canceled—or that every seat is gone forever. It may mean the current allocation is exhausted.

Before the onsale, verify the event on the venue’s own calendar. Write down the local time and open the official link early. During a high-demand sale, searching for a link after the queue begins is an excellent way to become a spectator.

When you reach checkout, compare the final total with the listing details. Look for service fees, delivery, taxes, currency conversion, and restrictions. The first number you see is an invitation to continue, not a promise about what hits your card.

## 3. Official resale or face-value exchange

Official resale exists for tickets that were already issued and are being returned to the marketplace under that seller’s rules. Depending on the event, tickets may be resold at the original price, at a permitted adjusted price, or under a venue-specific exchange policy.

The important word is “official.” Follow the resale link from the original ticket account, venue, artist, or primary seller. Do not assume that a page using the event’s name is connected to the event.

Read the delivery and refund terms before paying. A resale ticket may be transferred immediately, later, or only through the seller’s mobile app. A listing can be legitimate and still be a poor fit if the delivery timing does not work for your trip.

## 4. Secondary marketplace listing

Secondary marketplaces let current ticket holders list inventory for other buyers. They can be useful when the primary sale is closed, but they add another layer of seller rules, delivery timing, fees, and buyer protection.

Compare like with like. “Lower bowl” is not a seat. Check the section, row, quantity, restricted-view notice, ticket type, and delivery method. A cheap-looking listing can become less attractive after fees—or turn out to be a VIP package, hospitality product, parking pass, or speculative listing rather than a standard admission ticket.

Never pay a stranger by gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or an off-platform method because they promise a better deal. If the transaction leaves the marketplace’s documented checkout and protection process, you are taking on a very different risk.

## What the RAYE scramble does—and does not—prove

The RAYE story shows how registration volume can overwhelm a small event. It does not prove that every registered fan was a bot, that every resale listing is fraudulent, or that one buying route is always cheapest. Ticketing systems are event-specific, and the rules on the official event page are the rules that matter.

It also shows why a single search result is not enough. One page may describe the announcement, another may collect registrations, another may list resale inventory, and the venue page may contain the authoritative date and access instructions. Compare the event facts before comparing the checkout options.

## A four-question checkout test

Before you pay, ask:

1. Am I on a page linked by the artist, venue, promoter, or authorized seller?
2. Is this a standard ticket, resale ticket, VIP package, hospitality product, or parking pass?
3. What is the all-in total, including delivery, taxes, and fees?
4. When and how will the ticket be delivered, and what happens if the event changes?

If the answer to any question is unclear, pause. A queue creates urgency; it does not remove the need to read.
