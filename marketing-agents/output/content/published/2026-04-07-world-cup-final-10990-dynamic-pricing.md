# FIFA Just Pushed a World Cup Final Ticket to $10,990. Here's How to Not Get Played.

Last week, FIFA opened the "Last-Minute Sales Phase" for the 2026 World Cup. They also quietly raised the price of a Category 1 final ticket by **38%** — to **$10,990**. That's the most expensive ticket in the history of the sport. Face value. From the league office. Not a scalper.

The opener at SoFi? Top seats are running about **$2,985**. A group-stage match in a smaller US host city? Still hundreds of dollars more than they were six weeks ago.

FIFA calls this "variable pricing." You and I know it by its other name: **dynamic pricing** — the same model that turned Bruce Springsteen and Oasis presales into PR disasters. The pitch is always the same: "prices reflect demand." The reality is: if you blink, the price moves, and it almost never moves down.

So here's the consumer-advocate version of what's actually going on, and what to do about it.

## What changed on April 1

Three things happened at once:

1. **The Last-Minute Sales Phase went live.** First-come, first-served, running through the end of the tournament. No more lottery, no more queues — but also no more fixed prices.
2. **Official resale reopened on April 2.** FIFA's own resale platform is now competing with StubHub and SeatGeek for the same seats.
3. **Category 1 prices jumped across the board.** The final hit $10,990. The opener crossed $2,900. Even mid-tier group games saw double-digit percentage increases.

More than 500 million ticket requests came in during the lottery phase alone. About 6 million tickets total will be sold across the tournament. The math is brutally simple: demand is roughly 80x supply, and FIFA built a pricing engine to capture every dollar of it.

## The dirty secret of "variable pricing"

Here's the thing nobody at FIFA wants to say out loud: **once face value goes dynamic, the resale market often becomes the cheaper option.**

That sounds backwards. It isn't. When the primary seller is constantly repricing toward the ceiling, secondary marketplaces — where actual humans are trying to offload tickets they can't use — frequently undercut them. We've seen it on Ticketmaster Platinum. We've seen it on Verified Resale. And we're already seeing it for World Cup group-stage matches in cities like Kansas City, Philadelphia, and Seattle.

The catch: you have to actually compare. In real time. Across every marketplace. Which is, conveniently, the entire reason TicketScan exists.

## How to play this without getting fleeced

Three rules. That's it.

### 1. Compare every match, every day

Don't assume FIFA is cheapest just because they're official. Don't assume StubHub is cheapest just because it's the biggest resale platform. Pull up the match on TicketScan and look at all three (Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, StubHub) side by side. The cheapest source flips constantly — sometimes intraday.

### 2. Set a price alert and walk away

The single biggest mistake we see fans make is checking prices three times a day, getting emotionally attached, and panic-buying at the top. Set a target price on your watchlist, close the tab, and let us email you when somebody — primary or resale — actually hits it. Most matches outside the final and the opener will see at least one meaningful dip between now and June 11.

### 3. Watch the host-city math

Tickets to matches in Mexico City and Toronto are pricing very differently from matches at MetLife or AT&T Stadium. Travel costs matter, but so does the local fan economy. Some of the best value in the entire tournament right now is in the **non-glamour US host cities** — the ones where corporate buyers aren't camping the resale market.
