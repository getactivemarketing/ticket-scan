## Psychology-driven micro-optimization

### Today's principle: Anchoring

**Rotation so far:** Commitment/Consistency (Apr 8), Social Proof (Apr 10), Scarcity (Apr 11), Loss Aversion (Apr 12), Reciprocity (Apr 13), FOMO (Apr 14). Today: Anchoring.

**Where:** Compare page -- the moment a user is looking at side-by-side prices across sources

**The problem:** The compare page shows prices from Ticketmaster, SeatGeek, and StubHub next to each other. Right now they're displayed in alphabetical order by source. There's no visual hierarchy that makes one price feel like a deal or another feel expensive. The user scans left to right with no anchor point, so the cheapest price doesn't register as a "find."

**Current state:** Three price cards in a row. Source name, price, and a link. Clean, functional, but psychologically neutral. There's no framing that says "here's what you'd pay at the expensive end" vs. "here's the deal."

**Proposed change:** Reorder compare results to show the highest price first.

Show prices from highest to lowest. The first price the user sees becomes the mental anchor. When they scan to the cheapest option, the gap feels larger than it would if they saw the cheap price first. Add a subtle "savings" callout on the lowest price:

```
StubHub: $247  |  Ticketmaster: $195  |  SeatGeek: $168 -- Save $79 vs highest
```

The "$79 savings" is math the user can already do, but seeing it written out activates loss aversion on top of anchoring. They think: "If I don't buy the cheap one, I'm wasting $79."

For cases where all three sources are within 5% of each other:
```
All three sources priced within $12 of each other. Pick the one you trust.
```

This honesty builds credibility. Not every comparison produces a winner, and pretending otherwise erodes trust.

**Why this works:**
- Anchoring bias: the first number you see distorts your evaluation of every number after it. A $247 ticket makes $168 feel like a steal, even if $168 is the normal market price.
- The "save $X" framing layers loss aversion (from Apr 12) on top of the anchor.
- The "priced within $X" honesty message builds trust for the times where there IS a large gap, because the user knows we're not manufacturing urgency every time.
- Daniel Kahneman's research showed that arbitrary anchors (even random numbers) shift estimates by 30-50%. Here the anchor isn't arbitrary -- it's a real price someone would pay without comparing.

**Implementation:**
- Sort compare results by `min_price` descending instead of alphabetical
- Add a `SavingsBadge` component that calculates highest - lowest
- For gaps < 5%, show the "priced within $X" message instead
- Track: does sorting by price change the click-through rate on the cheapest source?

**Expected impact:**
- Click-through on cheapest source: +20-30% (anchoring makes the savings feel bigger)
- User perception of TicketScan's value: higher (they "found" a deal through comparison)
- Return visits: marginal improvement (users who feel they got a deal come back to check again)
